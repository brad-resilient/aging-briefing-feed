Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Thursday, October fifteenth. Medicare open enrollment opens today and runs to December seventh. Three things. UnitedHealth just showed how insurers get profitable in Medicare Advantage right now: by shrinking. A Kansas foundation is paying to rip out pull cords in rural senior housing and replace them with a connected safety net. And new research says the best way to help a dementia caregiver may be to help the person they're caring for. Today's learning segment is about family caregivers.

First, UnitedHealth's third quarter, reported Tuesday. Profit was four point one billion dollars, up seventy five percent from a year ago, on flat revenue. The share of premiums spent on medical care fell to about eighty eight percent from almost ninety. How did they do it? UnitedHealthcare expects to lose more than a million Medicare Advantage members this year, after cutting plans and benefits. For 2027 it cut hundreds more plans and trimmed benefits again, while expanding special needs plans, the plans built for people with chronic conditions or who are on both Medicare and Medicaid. Its share of members in four star plans drops from eighty one percent to about sixty eight percent next year. The message to the market is that margin comes first and membership second. Why this matters to you: the growth inside Medicare Advantage is moving toward special needs plans. Those plans serve the sickest, most expensive members, and they're required to do care coordination. That's where monitoring has the clearest return.

Second, rural Kansas. The Patterson Family Foundation gave three hundred thousand dollars to put a connected safety net into rural senior housing, the subsidized apartment buildings funded by the USDA and the HUD Section 202 program. A company called Safely Living is replacing old pull cords with wireless call buttons that connect to a twenty four hour response line, plus outreach calls to residents twice a month. A virtual occupational therapy company called AskSAMIE will do home safety evaluations, with up to two thousand dollars of equipment installed per resident. The target is at least one hundred evaluations in a year. In Wilson County, where it starts, about seventy one percent of seniors live alone. The release says an ambulance trip averages about twenty two hundred dollars and cites a twenty seven percent drop in rehospitalizations from an earlier pilot, without details, so treat that number as unverified. Remember the opportunity from Monday, a safety layer for senior affordable housing? This is that idea, being funded by philanthropy right now. The open question is the same one: who pays when the grant runs out.

Third, a study from the University of Connecticut, in the Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, looked at spouses caring for a partner with dementia, and how their circle of support changes as the disease progresses. Friendships often faded without any conflict, just because invitations stopped. Support groups and the medical team became more important later on. And one finding stood out: caregivers counted help given to their spouse as help to themselves. A friend taking the person with dementia out to lunch was experienced as support for the caregiver. The authors argue for supporting the pair together. A separate small study of a Meals on Wheels program that paired older adults with young volunteers found less loneliness for some participants, but with only thirteen older adults it's preliminary.

On business, the pattern this week is consolidation and retreat by large insurers, and small philanthropic and state money funding the practical safety pilots. I didn't find a significant new aging tech funding round in the last two days.

On the social side, the steady drumbeat is caregiver burnout. Several 2026 surveys report that nine in ten family caregivers show signs of burnout, with younger caregivers hit hardest. I couldn't reach Reddit or Hacker News directly today, so this is from published surveys and coverage.

The Resilient angle. First, follow special needs plans. As Medicare Advantage shrinks overall, those plans are where insurers are still growing, and they have the sickest members and a mandate to coordinate care. A wearable that catches deterioration early is a natural fit there. Second, the Kansas project shows the entry point in senior housing is replacing pull cords, which everyone already accepts as a safety requirement. A wearable button that also measures vital signs and detects falls is an upgrade to something the building already pays for. Third, the caregiver research is a design lesson. If help for the person with dementia is felt as help by the caregiver, then a monitoring product should be sold to the caregiver as relief, such as getting a full night's sleep, not as surveillance.

Today's learning segment is family caregivers. AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving count about sixty three million family caregivers in America, roughly one in four adults, up forty five percent in a decade. The average caregiver is fifty one, and three in five are women. Forty four percent provide high intensity care, and thirty percent have been doing it for five years or more. One in five say their own health is fair or poor because of caregiving. Seventy percent of working age caregivers also hold a job. Almost half of caregivers under fifty are also raising children, the sandwich generation. The money hurts: more than a third have stopped saving, and almost a quarter have gone into debt because of caregiving. About eleven million get some form of payment, mostly through Medicaid programs that pay family members or through the VA. The unpaid care they provide is worth hundreds of billions of dollars a year, more than all paid home care combined. That's an AARP estimate and worth checking for the latest figure. Three things for you. First, the family caregiver is usually the buyer and the daily user of eldercare technology, even when the older adult wears it. Second, their biggest needs are sleep, time and fewer surprises, not more data. Third, some of the money now flows through programs that pay caregivers or fund respite, like Medicare's GUIDE dementia model and Medicaid self directed care, and those programs are potential payers.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: overnight peace of mind for dementia caregivers. Many spouses caring for someone with dementia barely sleep, because they're listening for wandering, falls or breathing problems in the night. The product is a wearable on the person with dementia, plus a bed or door sensor, that watches overnight and only wakes the caregiver when something is actually wrong, with a nurse line for escalation. The pitch is a full night's sleep for the caregiver, which in turn keeps the person with dementia at home longer. The payers could be dementia care programs in Medicare's GUIDE model, which already pay for caregiver support and respite, special needs plans focused on dementia, and families paying privately. The risks are false alarms that destroy trust, and people with dementia removing the device. The first test is to ask ten spousal caregivers in a dementia support group how many nights a week they're woken, and what they'd pay to sleep through.

A question to think about today: if the caregiver is the real customer, what would a product designed around the caregiver's sleep look like, instead of around the patient's vital signs? That's your briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
