Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Wednesday, October fourteenth. Medicare open enrollment starts tomorrow, so today leans on Medicare Advantage. Three things. Medicare Advantage is quietly disappearing from parts of rural America. A rural health system just put twenty million dollars into care at home. And the senior care industry's own awards show what operators are actually buying. It was a light news day otherwise, so the learning segment gets a little more room.

First, the map of Medicare Advantage for 2027. KFF published a county by county analysis last Thursday. The average person on Medicare still has twenty eight Medicare Advantage drug plans to choose from. But one hundred ninety three counties now have none at all, up from one hundred twenty three this year. That's about six hundred thirty eight thousand people, and all of Alaska. The big insurers are pulling out of counties by the hundreds. Health Care Service Corporation, the Blue Cross parent in Illinois and Texas, leaves about five hundred counties. Centene leaves about three hundred sixty, and UnitedHealth about two hundred thirty. UnitedHealth now covers about seventy percent of counties, down from nearly eighty. Molina and Clear Spring are leaving the drug plan market entirely. Separately, Becker's counts twelve insurers leaving Medicare Advantage completely next year, and eight of them are owned in whole or in part by health systems. Providence, Memorial Hermann, Sharp, Boston Medical Center's WellSense and UMass Memorial's plan are all on that list. The one big exception is Devoted Health, which is entering three hundred forty two new counties without leaving any. Why this matters to you: health systems that ran their own Medicare Advantage plans were natural buyers of monitoring, because they kept the savings when patients stayed out of the hospital. Many are now walking away from that bet. Devoted, which runs its own care teams, is going the other way and needs care capacity in hundreds of new places.

Second, Dartmouth Health announced Dartmouth Health at Home, a twenty million dollar effort to expand home based primary care and palliative care across rural northern New England, with support for family caregivers built in. The chair of medicine described a full team serving patients and their families at home, and the stated goal is a financially sustainable model other systems could copy. They haven't said how it gets paid for over the long term, and that is exactly the open question. Rural home care has terrible travel economics. A clinician who spends half the day driving can see only a few patients. So anything that lets a rural team know who needs a visit today and who is fine, without driving out to check, goes straight at the cost problem.

Third, McKnight's, the long term care trade publication, handed out its technology awards last month, and the winners tell you what operators are buying. Top honor went to Jewish Home in New York for building AI tools in house. A company called Helpany won the falls prevention category with United Zion Retirement Community, claiming zero falls with AI monitoring. And a company called Zemplee won two categories for a Kansas project the organizers called nursing homes without walls. It's passive sensors in the home, no cameras and no wearables, working with the Kansas Department of Aging and Disability Services and a Medicaid health plan to keep people who qualify for nursing home care living at home. Zemplee's own materials say the Medicaid home care funding from the pandemic relief law ends this December, which is worth checking, because it could squeeze programs like this one. All of these results are self reported by the winners, not trials.

On research, nothing major was published in the last two days that I could confirm. One thread worth knowing keeps resurfacing: frailty and falls appear to show up years before a dementia diagnosis. A University of Queensland study this summer linked frailty to dementia onset two to three years earlier, and an older Brigham study linked a fall to higher odds of a dementia diagnosis within a year. If that holds up, gait, strength and fall data from a wearable could be an early warning signal for cognitive decline, not just a safety alarm. That's an inference, and the evidence is still observational.

The Resilient angle. First, follow the capitated money. The buyers who profit when an older person stays well are Medicare Advantage plans that own care delivery, like Devoted, plus PACE programs and state Medicaid home care programs. Health system owned plans are retreating, so they're weaker targets for now. Second, rural is where the economics of monitoring are strongest, because every avoided drive is real money, and it's also where Medicare Advantage is thinning out. Third, Zemplee and Helpany show that sensor based monitoring wins awards when it's packaged as a program with a partner, a payer and a result, not sold as a device.

Today's learning segment is Medicare Advantage one oh one. Original Medicare pays doctors and hospitals fee for service. Medicare Advantage is the alternative, where a private insurer takes a fixed monthly payment per member from the government and takes on the risk of that member's costs. A little over half of people on Medicare are now in these plans. The payment starts from a county benchmark tied to what traditional Medicare spends there. Plans bid against that benchmark. If they bid below it, part of the difference comes back as a rebate that has to be spent on members, which is what pays for dental, vision, gym memberships, and services like in home help and monitoring. Payments are then adjusted up or down for each member's diagnoses, which is called risk adjustment, and plans with four stars or more get a quality bonus on top. So a plan makes money three ways: managing care so it costs less than the payment, documenting how sick members are, and earning stars. Why are plans pulling back now? Since 2023, older people have been using more care than plans expected. Medicare has phased in a new risk adjustment model that pays less for some diagnoses. Drug cost changes from the Inflation Reduction Act shifted more of the bill onto plans. Star ratings got harder. And the 2027 rate increase, about two and a half percent, didn't keep up with costs. So insurers are cutting their least profitable counties, trimming extra benefits, and chasing margin, with Humana aiming for at least a three percent margin by 2028. For you, the lesson is that a Medicare Advantage plan buys things that cut medical costs within a year or two, or that move a specific star measure. Nice to have benefits are what gets cut first.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: a rural home care team in a box. Health systems like Dartmouth are trying to build home based care in rural areas, while Medicare Advantage plans leave those same counties. The product bundles a wearable and simple home sensors, a daily automated check in, and a remote nurse who triages, so the rural team only drives out when the data says a visit is needed. It would be sold as a per patient per month service with a target, like fewer emergency visits or more patients per clinician. The buyers would be rural health systems building home programs, state Medicaid home care programs, and fast growing plans like Devoted that just entered hundreds of new counties without care teams on the ground. The risks are that philanthropy funded programs run out of money, and that rural connectivity is poor. The first test is to call Dartmouth Health at Home and two other rural home based programs and ask what share of home visits turn out to be unnecessary.

A question to think about today: if Medicare Advantage keeps retreating from rural counties, who becomes the payer that cares about keeping a rural eighty five year old out of the hospital? That's your briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
