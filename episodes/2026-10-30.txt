Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Friday, October thirtieth. Three things today. A top Medicare official gave the first real numbers on the freeze on new home health and hospice agencies, and hinted at how it might change after November thirteenth. Medicare is finally forcing an insurer to pay back alleged overbilling, with Elevance wiring three hundred forty two million dollars. And a new AARP study says family caregivers now spend more than eleven thousand dollars a year of their own money. Today's learning segment is a market close to Resilient's own: medical alert systems, the button you press when you fall.

First, the freeze. Home Health Care News reports that Kim Brandt, CMS's chief operating officer, told the home care industry's annual meeting that the national moratorium has blocked two hundred fifty new home health agencies and fifty new hospices from enrolling in Medicare. She said it has caused large drops in billing in some areas, which she described as money that had been going to bad actors rather than good providers. On what comes next, she said the law only allows extensions in six month blocks, and that CMS has considered limiting the freeze to certain regions, or creating a gold card program that would exempt providers with strong compliance records. She gave no decision date. The trade group's lobbyist warned that a nationwide freeze could leave rural areas without enough home health. Why this matters: a gold card for clean providers would turn compliance records into a business asset, which fits the pattern we've been following all month.

Second, Medicare Advantage overbilling. KFF Health News reports that Elevance Health, which covers about two million Medicare members, wired three hundred forty two million dollars to CMS in May, after CMS threatened to stop new enrollment in its Medicare Advantage plans over what it called substantial and persistent problems with billing data and returning overpayments. It appears to be the first time CMS has pushed a Medicare Advantage plan into repaying an amount like that. Elevance says the action is unprecedented and is challenging it, and it estimated its broader exposure at about nine hundred thirty five million dollars. In January, Kaiser Permanente agreed to pay five hundred fifty six million to settle Justice Department claims that it billed for conditions patients didn't have. Experts called the Elevance payment small relative to its Medicare revenue, but a step in the right direction. Why this matters: between star rating lawsuits, overbilling repayments and plan exits, Medicare Advantage insurers are under pressure on every side. They'll keep paying for things that clearly improve quality scores or cut costs, and they'll be much more careful about anything that looks like it inflates diagnoses.

Third, the cost of caregiving. KFF's morning briefing summarizes an NPR report on a new AARP study: family caregivers of older, disabled or chronically ill adults now spend an average of about eleven thousand five hundred dollars a year out of pocket, roughly sixty percent more than five years ago, and about thirty one percent of their income. I couldn't open the NPR piece itself, so I'm relying on that summary. Also this week: a study reported by the Washington Post used a decades old British school leaving law and UK Biobank records to show that staying in school an extra year as a teenager was linked to lower dementia risk later in life. Modern Healthcare reports PACE programs are growing by tailoring services to Asian, Hispanic, Muslim and LGBTQ older adults. And Florida received a one hundred ninety four million dollar federal rural health award that includes remote monitoring and community paramedics, a reminder that rural health money is one place remote monitoring is still being funded.

On the social side, I couldn't reach Reddit or Hacker News directly, so nothing reliable to report.

The Resilient angle. Today's learning segment is the market closest to Resilient's own automated response devices, so I'll be direct. Medical alert is a mature, crowded, mostly consumer market where devices have become cheap and marketing is expensive. The opening isn't a better button. It's automatic detection that doesn't depend on the person pressing anything, proven with real outcome data, sold through payers who fund it, like Medicaid waiver programs and Medicare Advantage plans. And the AARP numbers suggest a separate, non device opportunity: families are spending heavily out of pocket and leaving money on the table, which is today's opportunity.

Today's learning segment is the medical alert market, also called personal emergency response systems. The classic product is a pendant or wristband with a button, connected to a base station or a cellular network, that calls a monitoring center staffed around the clock. Newer versions add automatic fall detection, GPS for use outside the home, and smartwatch styles. Typical prices, from memory, run roughly twenty five to sixty dollars a month, with fall detection often an extra charge. The market has consolidated. Connect America bought Philips' Lifeline business in 2021 and calls itself the largest independent medical alert provider. Best Buy paid eight hundred million dollars for GreatCall in 2018, later renamed Lively, which sells phones and alert devices to older adults. Other well known brands include Life Alert, Medical Guardian, Bay Alarm Medical and MobileHelp, and home security companies like ADT also sell alert services. That brand list is from general knowledge.

How do they make money? Mostly monthly subscriptions sold directly to families, with heavy spending on television and online advertising, so customer acquisition is expensive. The other big channel is payers. Many state Medicaid waiver programs cover medical alerts for frail members living at home. For example, CareSource's Ohio policy covers them for members receiving long term services at home if a provider documents the need, the person lives alone or with another impaired person, and the provider tests the unit monthly and staffs a center around the clock. Some Medicare Advantage plans also offer them as a supplemental benefit. Churn is structural: subscribers leave because they move into facilities, die, or stop seeing value, so companies are constantly replacing customers. That's a general industry pattern, from memory. The shutdown of 3G networks in 2022 and 2023 forced many older devices to be replaced, and disrupted some users.

Now, does it work? The honest answer: it depends on whether people use it. A Canadian study of people over eighty living alone with a medical alert found high satisfaction, about ninety percent said it helped them stay independent, but only eight of thirty participants had ever pressed the button in a real emergency. Some forgot they were wearing it. Some didn't wear it at home. Some didn't want to bother anyone, or waited to see if they'd feel better. A well known British study of people over ninety found that most who fell while alone and had an alarm didn't use it, which I'm citing from memory. That matters because lying on the floor for an hour or more after a fall, called a long lie, is linked to dehydration, pressure injuries, pneumonia and worse outcomes. So the core weakness of the button is the human decision to press it. Automatic fall detection addresses that, but as we discussed this week, it misses slow falls and produces false alarms. The best opportunity for a new entrant is reliable automatic detection plus a response service that people trust, with data showing fewer long lies and fewer hospital stays.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: a caregiver benefits recovery service. The customer is the family caregiver, reached through employers as a workplace benefit, or directly. The problem: AARP says caregivers are spending about eleven thousand five hundred dollars a year out of pocket, a third of their income, while many don't claim benefits they may qualify for, such as paid family caregiver programs under Medicaid, veterans' Aid and Attendance pensions, long term care insurance claims that are hard to file, tax credits and dependent care accounts, and state respite grants. The service would screen each family for every program the older adult and caregiver might qualify for, prepare and file the applications and insurance claims, and handle appeals. Who pays: employers, as a caregiving benefit to reduce absenteeism and turnover; or families, through a modest fee or a share of first year benefits recovered, where that's legal. Why now: caregiving costs up sixty percent in five years, Medicaid cuts and eligibility checks making paperwork harder, and employers looking for caregiving benefits. Risks: some benefit programs restrict fees for helping with claims, especially veterans' benefits, so the model has to be checked program by program, and employer caregiving benefits are a crowded space. No hardware is needed. The first test is to take ten caregiving families in Pittsburgh, screen them, and measure how many dollars of unclaimed benefits you find per family.

A question to think about this weekend: if most people with a medical alert button don't press it when they fall, what would you have to build so that help comes anyway? That's your briefing. Have a good weekend.
