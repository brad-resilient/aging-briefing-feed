Third, the Indiana audit. Skilled Nursing News reports that the HHS inspector general audited Medicaid supplemental payments for nursing homes in Indiana. These are extra payments, on top of normal Medicaid rates, meant to help nursing homes. Indiana got about one point zero two billion dollars of the two point three six billion paid nationwide in fiscal 2022. The catch: about ninety seven percent of Indiana nursing homes were operated under arrangements with county owned hospitals, and the auditors found two hundred five million dollars of the supplemental money was recouped by those county entities, mostly hospitals, rather than staying with the nursing homes. The inspector general wrote that federal oversight of Medicaid supplemental payments has long been a concern, and flagged twelve million dollars in claims that didn't follow federal rules. Why this matters: it's another example of money meant for care flowing elsewhere, the same theme as the transparency reports CMS just proposed to drop last week. Expect the new Congress to connect those dots.

Now the learning segment: Medicare Advantage star ratings. Most of this is from memory and general knowledge of the program, so treat the details as approximate.

What they are. Every year, CMS rates each Medicare Advantage contract from one to five stars, in half star steps, based on dozens of measures. They're published each October, and the stars released this fall, called the 2027 ratings, determine bonus payments in 2028. That's why Elevance says its alleged error will cost it five hundred sixteen million dollars in bonuses scheduled for 2028.

Why they matter so much. Plans rated four stars or higher get a quality bonus, from memory about five percent added to their payment benchmark, and double that in certain counties. Higher rated plans also keep a larger share of the money left over when they bid below the benchmark, called the rebate, which they use for extra benefits like lower premiums, dental or vision. Those benefits attract members. So the difference between three and a half and four stars can be worth hundreds of millions or even billions for a large insurer. That's why at least five insurers, including CVS, Centene and Elevance, have sued CMS over the 2027 ratings.

What gets measured. Roughly forty measures across several sources. Clinical quality measures from claims and medical records, like controlling blood pressure, diabetes care, breast cancer screening and follow up after emergency visits. Member surveys about experience, like getting needed care quickly and rating the plan. A health outcomes survey that asks members about physical and mental health, falls and physical activity. Drug plan measures, especially medication adherence for diabetes drugs, blood pressure drugs and statins. And administrative measures like call center performance, complaints and appeals. Different measures carry different weights, and CMS adjusts the weights and cut points almost every year, which is a big part of why insurers sue: a small change in a cut point can drop a plan below four stars.

Which measures can an outside company help move? From memory, the ones most often targeted are medication adherence, which pharmacists and coaches can improve; blood pressure control, where home readings and follow up help; follow up after hospital and emergency visits and smooth transitions of care; readmissions; and the survey based measures on falls, physical activity and care coordination, which depend on what members remember and feel about their care. CMS also tracks concurrent use of opioids and benzodiazepines, which ties back to the sedative safety idea from Monday. Plans pay vendors well for services that close specific gaps on specific measures, because the payoff is so large.

What it means for Resilient, and this is my analysis. Star measures are probably the most direct way a monitoring or care program gets paid by Medicare Advantage plans. But plans buy measurable gap closure, not technology. A pitch that says we will raise your blood pressure control rate and medication adherence among members in these senior living buildings, with a fee tied to results, is much easier for a plan to say yes to than a pitch about sensors.

Today's opportunity, and it's not a device. A star measure gap closure service delivered inside senior living. Many Medicare Advantage members live in senior apartment buildings and assisted living communities, where they're easy to reach in person. The service would put a pharmacist or trained coach on site a few days a month in partnering buildings to review medications and refill timing, take blood pressure readings that count for the measure, schedule overdue screenings, follow up after hospital stays, and run short falls and activity programs. Who pays: Medicare Advantage plans, per member and per gap closed, with bonuses tied to measure improvement; senior living operators get a free amenity that keeps residents healthier. Why now: insurers are fighting over every fraction of a star, ratings cut points keep moving, and plans need reliable in person ways to reach older members. Risks: each plan cares about only its own members, so a building has residents across many plans; data sharing between plans and the service takes work; and CMS may change measures again. The first test: partner with one Medicare Advantage plan with a large Pittsburgh membership and two or three senior apartment buildings, and measure changes in adherence, blood pressure control and screening completion over one year.

A question to think about: if a plan's bonus depends on whether members remember their care as well coordinated, is the most valuable service the one that actually coordinates care, or the one that makes sure members notice it? That's your briefing. Have a good Thursday.
