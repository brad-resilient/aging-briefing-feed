Senate Democrats have opened a serious conversation about a home care benefit in Medicare, the thing that Medicare has never covered. Medicare is getting tougher on providers, and payers are demanding faster, more accountable home care. And new federal data show deaths from falls among older adults keep rising, while a big review finds that most fall detection technology has never proven it improves outcomes. Today's learning segment is falls: how big the problem is, what it costs, and what actually prevents them.

First, a Medicare home care benefit. Democrats on the Senate Finance Committee published an eighty one page request for information last week, asking for input on a universal home care benefit under Medicare and Medicaid: what it should cover, how it would work with provider networks, how to deal with the caregiver shortage, and how to make eligibility more consistent across states. It follows the Medicare at Home Act, introduced in August, which would add help with daily activities like bathing and dressing, plus things like meal preparation and light housekeeping, to Medicare Part B. Home care trade groups welcomed it. To be clear, this is a request for ideas from the minority party, not a bill on its way to passage, and there's no cost estimate. But it matters because the biggest gap in American elder care is that Medicare doesn't pay for long term help at home, and most families pay out of pocket or spend down to Medicaid. If a benefit like this ever passes, even in a limited form, it would be the largest new market in aging services in decades. Worth watching, and worth having a view on.

Second, accountability. Last week CMS gave itself the power to remove healthcare organizations from Medicare and Medicaid on its own authority, a power that previously sat only with the federal Inspector General, effective immediately. Hospitals worry about legal costs and say it's unclear how providers can appeal. Taken together with the fraud related payment suspensions and the hospice and home health enrollment freeze we've been covering, the direction is clear: compliance is now a competitive advantage. And payers are raising the bar on performance too. Home Health Care News reports that health systems and Medicare Advantage plans increasingly expect home health agencies to respond to a referral within seven to ten minutes and start care within twenty four hours or the same day. Agencies that can't offer every discipline, nursing, therapy and aides, risk being left out of preferred networks, and payers want granular data, like readmissions tied to medication errors. One agency, LifeCare, said an AI scribe cut the time for a first visit from about two and a half hours to forty five minutes, letting clinicians do four or five new patients a day instead of three. The lesson: speed, breadth and proof are what get home care providers into networks now.

Third, falls. The CDC's latest data show the age adjusted death rate from falls among adults sixty five and older rose twenty one percent between 2018 and 2024, from about sixty five to about seventy eight deaths per hundred thousand. More than fourteen million older adults, about one in four, report a fall every year. And a scoping review published this year in Frontiers in Public Health looked at two hundred forty three studies of fall detection and fall prediction technology for older adults. More than half tested only staged falls in a lab. Fewer than a third tested in real life with older adults. Wearable motion sensors that detect a fall after it happens are the most proven, but the review found no robust evidence that any of these technologies reduce injuries or improve quality of life, and almost no studies looked at cost effectiveness. False alarms, people not wearing devices, privacy concerns with cameras, and poor connection to who actually responds were the recurring problems.

The Resilient angle. Today is one where hardware genuinely belongs in the conversation, so let's be honest about it. Falls are a huge, growing, well funded problem, and detection devices are everywhere. But the evidence gap is the story: almost nobody has shown that detecting falls improves outcomes. That's actually where Resilient has a real edge, not the device itself, but the ability to run rigorous clinical studies. A company that can prove an outcome, fewer injuries, faster help, fewer hospital days, would stand out in a crowded market. The second thought: prevention beats detection. The strongest evidence is for exercise programs and medication review, which don't need hardware at all. Today's learning segment is falls. Start with scale. About one in four adults over sixty five falls each year, and falls are the leading cause of injury in that age group. Federal estimates put spending on non fatal falls at about eighty billion dollars a year. Death rates have risen for two decades: a CDC report found that from 2003 to 2023, fall death rates rose more than seventy percent for people sixty five to eighty four and more than doubled for people eighty five and older. Part of the rise reflects an older, frailer population, more people on medications that affect balance, and better reporting, so it's not purely a failure of care. What causes falls? Usually several things at once: weak legs and poor balance, medications like sedatives, sleeping pills, some antidepressants and blood pressure drugs, poor vision, foot problems, low blood pressure on standing, dementia, and hazards at home like rugs, poor lighting and stairs. Hip fractures are the most feared result, and many older adults who break a hip never regain their previous independence. That's a widely reported finding, but check exact figures before citing them. What works to prevent falls? The strongest evidence is for exercise that builds strength and balance, like the Otago program, tai chi, and community programs like Stepping On. Remember Sunday's trial, where a simple prompt in the electronic health record to prescribe home exercises cut falls about nine percent. Next is reviewing and reducing risky medications. Home safety changes help most for people at high risk, especially after a hospital stay, often delivered by occupational therapists. Treating vision problems, like cataract surgery, helps too. Vitamin D supplements alone generally don't prevent falls in people who aren't deficient. The CDC's STEADI program packages this into screen, assess and intervene for clinicians, and the federal government just funded its expansion. Detection technology, from pendants and smartwatches to radar and cameras, addresses a different problem: getting help quickly after a fall, since lying on the floor for a long time causes its own harm. That's valuable, but it's not prevention, and as the review showed, it hasn't yet proven better outcomes.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: a measurable fall prevention program sold to organizations that pay for the consequences. Medicare Advantage special needs plans, PACE programs and value based primary care groups all pay for the hospital stays and nursing home time that falls cause. The idea is a program that finds their high risk members, then delivers the proven pieces at home: an Otago style exercise program led by physical therapy assistants with video check ins, a pharmacist medication review focused on fall risk drugs, and an occupational therapy home safety visit with simple fixes like grab bars and lighting. It's priced per enrolled member, with part of the fee tied to measured reductions in falls and fall related emergency visits. Why now: fall deaths are rising, the federal government is funding prevention, condition focused plans are growing fast, and Sunday's trial showed even light touch programs work. Risks: proving reductions takes a year or more and good data, adherence to exercise fades, and therapy staff are scarce. Is hardware needed? Not for the core program. A detection device could be offered as an add on for the highest risk members, but the business shouldn't depend on it, and the review says it hasn't proven outcomes yet. Resilient's real fit here is its clinical study capability: designing the program so it produces credible outcome data from day one. The first test is to ask two Pennsylvania LIFE programs and one special needs plan what falls cost them per year, and what they'd pay for a program that reliably reduces them.

A question to think about: in a market full of devices that tell you someone has fallen, what would it take to be the company that proves fewer people fell? That's your briefing. Have a good Thursday.
