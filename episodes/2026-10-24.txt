Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Saturday, October twenty fourth. Three things today. The federal government updated its national plan for Alzheimer's and dementia, with more weight on prevention and on care at home. The crackdown on home care fraud got bigger again, with another one point two billion dollars in California Medicaid payments held back, plus a new six month window for providers to confess billing problems. And Medicaid is getting tighter for older people in ways that will push more costs onto families. Today's learning segment is loneliness and social isolation, how much it really hurts health, and what the evidence says about companion technology.

First, the dementia plan. On Thursday, the Department of Health and Human Services released an update to the National Plan to Address Alzheimer's Disease and Related Dementias. The plan now leans hard on reducing dementia risk across a lifetime, and on supporting care at home: caregiver support, respite, home and community based services, safety and planning. Alongside it, the FDA launched something it calls the Alzheimer's Prevention Accelerator, to speed up products aimed at brain diseases, and ARPA H, the government's high risk research agency, committed up to forty eight point eight million dollars to earlier detection and intervention. CMS chief Mehmet Oz pointed to the GUIDE dementia model as the payment side of all this. Why it matters: this fits what we covered yesterday. Federal policy is moving toward finding dementia earlier and keeping people at home longer. Plans like this don't come with much new money by themselves, but they shape grant programs and pilots for years, and they make prevention and home based dementia support easier to sell to payers.

Second, the fraud crackdown. Home Health Care News reports that CMS has held back another one point two billion dollars in federal Medicaid payments to California for personal home care services. That comes on top of more than a billion dollars held back in July from California and Minnesota. Oz says California's personal care spending grew more than twenty four percent in three years, about double other states, and that the fraud effort has at least two more years to run. California Senator Alex Padilla said the move will hurt older Californians and people with disabilities. At the same time, HHS and the Justice Department opened a six month pilot, running until April twenty second next year, that lets health care companies, including home care agencies, voluntarily report False Claims Act problems and settle them in one coordinated deal. Industry groups like the idea but worry about the cost. Under the False Claims Act, repayment can be three times the damages plus penalties, which could sink a small agency. And the pilot only covers civil billing problems, not kickbacks, which are criminal. Put this together with last week's news that CMS can now exclude providers on its own, the national freeze on new home health and hospice agencies, and the adult day center actions in New York, and the picture is clear. In home and community care, being able to prove you're clean is becoming a competitive advantage. That's today's opportunity.

Third, the Medicaid squeeze. CalMatters reports that California will cut the Medi Cal asset limit for older adults and people with disabilities to twenty one thousand dollars, starting in July 2027. The state got rid of its asset test entirely in 2024, brought it back this year at one hundred thirty thousand dollars, and is now cutting it again to close a budget gap. A home and one car still don't count, but savings, some retirement accounts and life insurance do. One woman with a disability told the paper that without Medi Cal she'd likely end up in a nursing home. Separately, KFF Health News reports that since October first, hundreds of thousands of lawfully present immigrants, including refugees, no longer qualify for Medicaid under last year's federal tax law. That matters for home care, because immigrants are a big share of the direct care workforce and their families. And on the insurance side, Modern Healthcare reports six states plan to raise Medicaid rates paid to Molina next year, by an average of about seven percent, to cover the cost of work requirements and more frequent eligibility checks. I couldn't open that article, so treat the detail as a headline. Finally, as we covered Wednesday, Humana and UnitedHealthcare are dropping Medicare Advantage plans covering close to a million people for next year. The common thread: public coverage for long term care is getting narrower, and the gap lands on families' savings and time.

On the technology side, I didn't find any major new launches or clearances worth your time this week, so the technology discussion moves into today's learning segment on companion devices.

On the social side, I couldn't reach Reddit or Hacker News directly, so nothing reliable to report.

The Resilient angle. Three thoughts. First, regulators are now treating home and community care the way the FDA treats medical devices: show your records or don't get paid. Resilient already lives in a world of quality systems, documentation and audits, and that habit is rare among small home care agencies. Second, as Medicaid tightens, more middle and lower income families will be paying for care themselves or doing it themselves, so lower cost services that help families get more out of fewer paid hours will sell. Third, on dementia, the national plan points money toward prevention and home support. Neither of those needs a device to start.

Today's learning segment is loneliness and social isolation. First, two words that sound alike but aren't. Social isolation is objective: living alone, few contacts, little time with others. Loneliness is the feeling that your connections aren't enough. You can be isolated and fine, or surrounded by people and lonely. Both matter for health. The World Health Organization's commission on social connection reported last year that loneliness is linked to more than eight hundred seventy thousand deaths a year worldwide, about one hundred an hour, and that up to one in three older adults are socially isolated. Lonely people are about twice as likely to develop depression, and loneliness and isolation are linked to more heart disease, stroke, diabetes and cognitive decline. In the US, the Surgeon General's 2023 advisory famously compared the mortality risk of social disconnection to smoking up to fifteen cigarettes a day. That comparison comes from one research group's analysis and I'm quoting it from memory, so use it carefully. For older adults, the triggers are predictable: losing a spouse, stopping driving, hearing loss, moving, and caregiving itself, which isolates the caregiver.

Now, what works. The honest answer is that the evidence for most interventions is modest. The better supported approaches are ones with a purpose and a group: volunteering, group activities built around shared interests, and treating hearing loss, which is one of the most fixable risk factors. Simple befriending, like regular phone calls, helps some people some of the time. Then there's the business side. Medicare Advantage plans have paid companies like Papa to send companions into members' homes, often weekly visits of a couple of hours, paid per member per month. KFF Health News reported that more than sixty five plans had signed on, but also that companions were asked to encourage health assessments that raise what Medicare pays the plan, so the motives are mixed. With plans cutting back on extras this year, those benefits are under pressure.

Companion technology is the newest piece. ElliQ is a tabletop robot that starts conversations, reminds people about medication, and connects them to family. It costs about forty dollars a month plus a two hundred fifty dollar start fee, and New York State's Office for the Aging has given it to hundreds of older adults. The company and the state reported that loneliness fell by ninety five percent in that program. But that figure comes from the company and its partner, with no comparison group and no published method, so it's marketing, not evidence. Engagement was real, though: users averaged more than thirty interactions a day. At MIT, researchers are running a study of two hundred twenty five adults over sixty five talking to an AI companion for a month, funded by the Retirement Research Foundation, to ask whether chatbots add to human relationships or replace them. Results aren't out yet. My summary: companion tech probably helps some lonely people feel better day to day, the strong proof isn't there yet, and the open question is whether it pulls people toward other humans or away from them.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: a compliance and billing audit service for small home and community care providers, starting in Pennsylvania. The customer is a small Medicaid home care agency, adult day center or hospice, with maybe fifty to five hundred clients and no compliance department. The problem: regulators are holding back payments, excluding providers and auditing claims, and most small agencies can't prove their visits, hours and documentation are clean. The service would do mock audits that compare visit records and caregiver timesheets to claims, find the gaps, set up simple ongoing monitoring and staff training, and, working with outside health care lawyers, help agencies decide whether to use the federal self disclosure window before it closes in April. Who pays: the agencies, as a monthly subscription after a paid first audit. Buyers in mergers would pay for the same review before closing a deal, and state associations or Medicaid managed care plans might sponsor it for their networks. Why now: enforcement is at a peak, there's a deadline, and compliance has become the first thing buyers look at. Risks: compliance consulting is crowded, small agencies have thin margins, and anything close to legal advice has to go through lawyers. No hardware is needed at all. Resilient's fit is its quality systems and audit culture, not its devices. The first test is to call five Pennsylvania home care or adult day operators and the state homecare association, and ask what an outside audit would be worth to them before April.

A question to think about this weekend: when regulators make proof the price of getting paid, who in elder care is best placed to sell that proof? That's your briefing. Enjoy your weekend.
