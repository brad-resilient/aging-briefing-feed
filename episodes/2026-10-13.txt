Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Tuesday, October thirteenth. Three things today. Medicare Advantage star ratings came out and shifted who has money to spend. Medicare's freeze on new home health and hospice agencies reaches a decision point next month. And a big review of AI for aging at home finds the technology is running well ahead of the evidence.

First, star ratings. Every fall, Medicare rates Medicare Advantage plans from one to five stars on quality. Plans at four stars or above earn bonus payments, worth about thirteen billion dollars a year across the industry. The 2027 ratings came out Friday. About seventy one percent of enrollees are in plans rated four stars or higher. Humana was the big winner, with ninety five percent of its members in four star plans or better, and its stock jumped about fifteen percent. UnitedHealthcare went the other way, dropping from about eighty one percent to about sixty seven percent according to J.P. Morgan. Only fifteen plans earned five stars, down from twenty two, and Alignment Healthcare, which was downgraded, is threatening to sue. Why this matters to you: bonus money pays for the extra benefits and care management programs that buy senior care services. Plans that just lost stars will be looking for partners who can move specific quality measures.

Second, the home health freeze. Since May, Medicare has blocked new home health agencies and hospices from enrolling anywhere in the country, citing fraud. The freeze also blocks some ownership changes, which chills acquisitions. It comes up for renewal on November thirteenth, and Medicare can extend it six months at a time. Industry groups want it to end. One number from them stuck with me: thirty eight percent of Medicare patients who were referred to home health after a hospital stay never got it. So home care capacity is capped twice, by regulation and by a shortage of workers. Anything that lets an existing agency safely serve more patients becomes more valuable.

Third, the evidence gap. A systematic review published this month looked at one hundred and one studies of AI for helping older people stay at home. Its conclusion is that technical progress has consistently outpaced proof that these systems help real older adults in real homes over meaningful periods. Health plans, Medicaid programs and health systems increasingly want outcomes, not demos. A company that runs a credible real home study has an edge most competitors don't have.

In technology news, a startup called Kindable launched an app called Loved. An AI caller named Claire phones an older person every day to ask about mood, sleep, appetite and medications. It then sends a summary to the family and turns needs like rides or chores into tasks. The older person doesn't need a smartphone, a wearable or a camera, just a regular phone. It joins a fast growing group of AI phone check-in services, which tells me the technology itself is becoming a commodity. The winners will be whoever owns the relationship and the contract with the payer. Also, over the weekend, Stanford hosted an aging tech event during San Francisco Tech Week. There were panels on sensing wellbeing from a distance and on home robotics, with early stage companies like Luffu, Care Mojo, Greenlue and Nurox Robotics. I've added them to the watch list.

On research, coverage this week resurfaced a study from rural South Africa in which older adults with measured hearing loss had about two and a half times the odds of dementia. It adds to the evidence that hearing is one of the biggest risk factors for dementia that we can actually change. It's also a practical reminder that any voice based product has to work for people who don't hear well.

On policy, the Medicaid cuts passed last year are starting to land. Roughly a trillion dollars comes out of Medicaid over ten years. As of October first, most lawfully present immigrants lost Medicaid eligibility. Starting next year, retroactive coverage shrinks and work reporting rules arrive for expansion adults. Advocates single out home and community based services as especially at risk, because those are optional benefits that states can cut when budgets get tight.

On the social side, it was a quiet couple of days, with no major new conversations beyond the themes from Sunday. The Stanford event put the core tension well: families want reassurance, and older adults want privacy and independence.

The Resilient angle. Star ratings give health plans a concrete reason to pay for monitoring. Several measures that feed the ratings, like falls risk, medication adherence and follow-up after hospital discharge, are things a monitoring program could plausibly improve. That's my inference, and it's worth checking the exact 2027 list. A plan that just lost stars is a motivated buyer. Second, with home health capacity frozen and short of staff, remote physiological monitoring that lets one nurse safely cover more patients after discharge addresses a real bottleneck. And third, evidence is the moat. Resilient already knows how to run clinical studies, and almost nobody in aging tech has real home outcome data.

Today's learning segment is who pays for elder care. Medicare pays for doctors, hospitals, drugs and short term recovery care. That means up to a hundred days in a skilled nursing facility after a qualifying hospital stay, plus part time skilled home health. It does not pay for long term custodial care, the everyday help with bathing, dressing, eating and supervision. That's the single biggest surprise for most families. Medicaid, which is run by states and is means tested, is the largest single payer of paid long term care, covering nursing homes and home and community based services. But people usually qualify only after spending down their savings, and the home based part is optional for states, which is why there are waitlists and why it's first in line for cuts. People who are on both Medicare and Medicaid, called dual eligibles, are the highest need group, and special plans and PACE programs serve them. Private long term care insurance is a small and shrinking part of the picture. And most care is actually paid out of pocket or given for free by family. For you, the payer you pick shapes everything. Medicaid means state politics and slow buying cycles. Medicare Advantage means star ratings and cost savings. Private pay means affluent families, where the buyer is usually the adult child.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: a post discharge home bridge. Remember that thirty eight percent of patients referred to home health never receive it. The idea is a thirty day kit handed out at discharge. It would include a wearable that tracks vital signs and breathing, a daily AI phone check-in, and a nurse who only gets involved when something looks wrong, so one nurse can cover many patients. Hospitals would pay to avoid readmission penalties. Medicare Advantage plans would pay because care after discharge counts toward their quality ratings. And home health agencies would pay because it stretches their nurses. The first test would be asking two hospital case management directors how many patients go home without home health starting within a week, and what happens to them.

A question to think about today: if you could only sell to one payer for the first two years, Medicare Advantage plans, Medicaid, hospitals or families, which would you pick and why? That's your briefing. Talk to you tomorrow.
