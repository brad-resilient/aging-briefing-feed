Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Wednesday, November eleventh, Veterans Day. Three things today. The freeze on new home health and hospice agencies ends in two days, and a top CMS official has said the agency is weighing a narrower version. Medicaid and Medicare Advantage fights are moving into the courts, with four states suing over Medicaid work requirements and Elevance suing over its star ratings. And a new national nursing home report finds staffing creeping up but depression among long term residents rising. Today's learning segment is how the FDA draws the line between a wellness gadget and a medical device, which matters directly for Resilient, especially after the agency loosened its wearables policy in January.

First, the freeze. The national moratorium on new Medicare enrollment of home health agencies and hospices took effect May thirteenth for six months, so it lapses Friday unless CMS publishes an extension. Home Health Care News reported late last month on comments from Kim Brandt, CMS's deputy administrator and chief operating officer. She said the freeze has blocked about two hundred fifty home health agencies and about fifty hospices from enrolling. She said CMS is looking at limiting it to certain areas and at a gold card exception for established, compliant providers, and is working with its lawyers on what she called a balanced solution. She also noted that by law any moratorium runs in six month blocks. As of yesterday I found no announcement. My read: the most likely outcome is an extension limited to the highest fraud states, like the ones CMS named in May, Arizona, California, Nevada and Texas, possibly with some exceptions. A clean national lapse would invite a rush of new applications, which CMS has clearly been trying to avoid.

Second, the legal fights. According to KFF Health News's Tuesday briefing, Minnesota, New Mexico, Oregon and Washington have sued over the Medicaid work requirements in last year's budget law, arguing they violate the Constitution's spending clause. KFF Health News also reported that Nebraska rushed its work requirement rules, budgeting eight point two million dollars for contractors, and that at least one cancer patient was told he'd lost coverage. That follows the federal court ruling we covered last week that struck down CMS's narrow definition of medically frail. On the Medicare Advantage side, Elevance Health sued CMS last Friday over its 2027 star ratings, saying the ratings cost it five hundred sixteen million dollars in bonus payments, according to Modern Healthcare. CVS sued over its star ratings earlier this fall too. Star ratings drive billions in bonuses, which is why plans fight over single measures. And a bit of good news for rural states: Politico reports CMS announced a second ten billion dollar round of Rural Health Transformation money going out to all fifty states. That appears to be the base funding, while the competitive portion we discussed last week was still delayed as of that reporting.

Third, nursing homes. U.S. News and World Report released its 2027 nursing home ratings yesterday, covering more than fourteen thousand six hundred facilities. Skilled Nursing News reports the top rated homes had twenty one percent fewer hospital readmissions and nineteen percent more staffing per resident than average. Nationally, direct care nursing time inched up from twenty six and a half to twenty seven minutes per resident per day. A U.S. News researcher called that small increase encouraging. But depressive symptoms among long term residents rose from ten percent to thirteen percent in a year, which the report flagged as a troubling new trend. Separately, Skilled Nursing News reported that nearly half of nursing home residents have some visual impairment, with cataracts the leading preventable cause. Both point to a theme we keep hitting: a lot of what makes life worse in long term care is treatable, and simply isn't being treated.

Now the learning segment: how the FDA separates wellness products from medical devices. I'm working from two law firm summaries, Foley and Squire Patton Boggs, rather than the FDA documents themselves, so the details should be checked against the guidance before anyone relies on them.

The basic idea. Under the law, a product is a medical device if it's intended to diagnose, treat, cure, mitigate or prevent a disease. Intended use is judged mostly by what the company says about it, in labels, marketing and the app itself. The FDA's general wellness policy says it won't regulate low risk products that are only meant to support a healthy lifestyle, like tracking sleep, activity or stress, as long as they don't make disease claims.

What changed in January. On January sixth, 2026, the FDA updated its general wellness guidance and its clinical decision support software guidance, without the usual comment period. According to Foley, this followed the FDA's 2025 warning letter to WHOOP over claims about physiologic measurements. The update makes clear that a non invasive wearable can estimate and display physiologic measures like blood pressure, blood oxygen and heart rate variability and still be a wellness product, if it meets several conditions. It can't be invasive or implanted. It can't claim to diagnose or treat any disease. It can't present itself as a substitute for an FDA cleared device. It can't prompt or guide specific medical decisions. And if it shows numbers that look like clinical values, those need to be validated. The product can show values, ranges, trends and baselines and relate them to sleep, activity, stress or recovery. It can tell the user that a check with a health professional may be worth it. But Foley's reading is that such a notification can't name a disease, can't call the reading abnormal or diagnostic, can't include clinical thresholds or treatment advice, and can't involve ongoing alerts for managing a disease. The two law firms describe the notification rules a bit differently, which is one more reason to read the FDA text.

What still needs FDA review. Anything that claims to screen for, diagnose, monitor, alert on, or manage a disease is a medical device. Most of those go through a five ten k, which shows the device is substantially equivalent to one already on the market. New kinds of moderate risk devices with no predecessor go through De Novo, which then creates a new category others can follow. Higher risk devices need premarket approval. Software that is itself the medical device is often called software as a medical device. The clinical decision support update also says that software analyzing continuous or streaming physiologic signals is generally a device function, while showing a single reading from a cleared device, like a blood pressure result, is not.

What this means for Resilient, and this is my analysis. There's now a clearer two track path. Track one is a wellness product: show breathing, oxygen and sleep trends to consumers or families, no disease claims, no alarms framed as medical emergencies. That can go to market faster, but it can't be billed to Medicare as remote patient monitoring, and it can't market itself as detecting overdoses or sleep apnea. Track two is a cleared medical device, which is what Medicare monitoring codes require, since remote patient monitoring billing requires a device that meets the FDA's definition of a medical device, from memory. Overdose or respiratory depression alerting is clearly track two. The strategic question is whether a wellness version could build a user base and real world data while the cleared version moves through review. The risk is that a wellness product's marketing drifts into medical claims, which is exactly what got WHOOP a warning letter.

Today's opportunity, and it's not a device. A depression care program for nursing home residents. With depressive symptoms in long term residents rising from ten to thirteen percent, there's a clear unmet need. The model is called collaborative care: a care manager, often a nurse or social worker, screens residents with a standard questionnaire, tracks symptoms, delivers brief therapy or behavioral activation, and reviews cases weekly with a consulting psychiatrist who advises the resident's doctor on medication. From memory, Medicare has billing codes for collaborative care that the treating clinician can bill monthly. The service company would supply the care managers, the psychiatrist and the tracking system to nursing homes and their attending physician groups, and share in the billing. Why now: the U.S. News data, quality measures that include depressive symptoms, staffing pressure that leaves no one with time for this, and a Democratic Congress focused on nursing home quality. Risks: residents with dementia are hard to assess, antidepressant use in frail people has real side effects, so the program has to lean on non drug approaches, and billing rules for nursing home residents are complicated. The first test: partner with one Pittsburgh area nursing home and its physician group, screen all long term residents, enroll those with symptoms for six months, and measure changes in depression scores, falls, hospital transfers and psychiatric medication use.

A question to think about: if a wearable can now legally show an older adult's blood oxygen trend as a wellness feature, but can't warn them it's dangerously low, who carries the responsibility when a family member sees a worrying number at night and doesn't know what to do?
