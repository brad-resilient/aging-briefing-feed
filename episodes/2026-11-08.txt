Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Sunday, November eighth. It's a quiet news weekend, so today is a short look back at a big week, two new items from Friday, and a longer learning segment on rural aging and access, which matters for western Pennsylvania. The business idea at the end is about bringing an all in one care program for frail seniors to rural counties.

First, the week in review. It was one of the busiest weeks of the year for aging policy. On Tuesday, voters gave Democrats control of both the House and the Senate, which means more oversight of Medicare Advantage, nursing homes and private equity, and a push to undo last year's Medicaid cuts, though President Trump can still veto. On Wednesday, Medicare finalized next year's physician payment rule. For remote monitoring, it requires a separate initiating visit, limits remote therapeutic monitoring to established patients, and finalized a ban on paying for monitoring done by outside contractors rather than a practice's own staff, but delayed that ban to 2029. The same rule gave accountable care organizations big incentives, including a thirty two percent bump in office visit pay for their doctors and permission to waive copays. Also Wednesday, Medicare finalized a two point four percent raise for home health, with tougher enrollment and revocation rules. Later in the week we heard from the companies. Addus said caregivers want forty hour weeks, so short personal care cases go unstaffed. Real estate trusts are pouring money into senior housing. Brookdale is moving away from paid referral services. And Pennant and PACS grew mostly by acquisition.

Now two new items from Friday. Home Health Care News took a closer look at the enforcement side of the home health rule. Beyond the raise, the rule adds new reasons Medicare can deny or revoke a company's enrollment, and widens when a revocation can be made retroactive. Providers worry that a late filing of a routine ownership or management change could be used to revoke enrollment back to an earlier date, which could mean paying back months of claims. The article also notes CMS attributed part of its payment math to sharp drops in billing in certain areas after the home health and hospice enrollment freezes. And Katie Smith Sloan, who leads LeadingAge, the association of nonprofit aging services providers, called CMS out of touch.

A correction on the freeze itself. In recent episodes I said the national freeze on new home health and hospice agencies expires around November thirteenth. This week I couldn't find a current source that confirms that date, so treat it as unconfirmed. What we do know from Home Health Care News is that about two hundred fifty agencies have been blocked from enrolling, and the options on the table include extending it, letting it lapse, limiting it to certain regions, or a gold card exemption for providers with clean records, which CMS hasn't announced.

Second, Senior Housing News looked at the middle market, meaning senior housing for people who have too much money for Medicaid but not enough for luxury communities. This is the biggest and fastest growing group of older adults. The article's conclusion is in its headline: capital and scale, not ideas, are the biggest barriers. A September report from the research firm Green Street found that an average new community, costing about three hundred fifty thousand dollars per unit to build, wouldn't make financial sense at middle market rents. One operator profiled, Mainstay, keeps rents between thirty six hundred and forty six hundred dollars a month. My read: the middle market won't be solved with new buildings alone. It points toward services that help people stay in their current homes longer, and toward lower cost models in existing buildings.

Now the learning segment: rural aging and access. Here are the core facts, mostly from the Rural Health Information Hub, a federally funded clearinghouse.

Rural America is older than urban America, because young people move away and retirees stay or move in. Western Pennsylvania is a good example. Most older adults want to stay put. An AARP survey found seventy one percent of rural adults want to remain in their communities as they age. But the services they'd need are thinner. The Rural Health Information Hub cites research finding urban areas have about thirty five percent more home health aides available than rural areas, and an older study found about ten percent of rural counties had become nursing home deserts after closures. Rural counties are also more likely to be what researchers call aging and disability services deserts. Area Agencies on Aging, the local offices that coordinate meals, transportation and in home help, report serious workforce problems in rural areas. In one survey, about three quarters cited staff burnout, competitive wages and staff shortages. Because of short staffing, sixty four percent of these agencies delayed starting services, and sixty percent started or extended waitlists.

Family caregivers carry most of the load. Among rural family caregivers, about half also work a job, more than a third live with the person they care for, and one in five has a disability themselves. And a growing number of older adults have no spouse, partner or child to help. A federal analysis estimated that share was about fourteen percent in 2020 and could reach about twenty six percent by 2080.

Technology helps less than you'd hope. About twenty eight percent of rural residents lack home broadband that meets the federal minimum speed, according to a 2024 FCC report. That limits video visits and anything that depends on a fast home connection. Transportation is a constant problem too. When the nearest specialist is an hour away and there's no bus, a missed ride means a missed appointment.

One more fact that matters for today's idea. More than a third of states have no PACE program at all, and states with more rural residents are less likely to have one. PACE stands for Program of All Inclusive Care for the Elderly. It serves people who are frail enough to qualify for nursing home care but want to live at home. A PACE organization takes a single monthly payment from Medicare and Medicaid and is responsible for all of a participant's care: doctors, nursing, therapy, home care, medications, meals, transportation and a day center. From memory, there are roughly a hundred fifty to two hundred PACE organizations nationally serving somewhere around eighty thousand people, so it's small, but outcomes and satisfaction tend to be good. The model is hard to run in rural areas because it's built around a day center that people are driven to, and rural distances make that expensive.

And remember the Rural Health Transformation Program, the fifty billion dollar fund we covered Friday. CMS missed its deadline to tell states their 2027 amounts, but each state's one hundred million dollar base is still coming. That's a possible source of startup money for rural aging programs, though I couldn't open the program's details today.

Today's opportunity, and it's mostly services, not devices. A rural PACE development and operations partner. Many rural health systems, critical access hospitals and county agencies would like to offer PACE but lack the capital, the know how to get through the federal and state approval process, and the scale to run it. The company would help them launch a smaller, rural version: a modest day center in an existing building like a hospital wing or a senior center, more care delivered at home and by video where broadband allows, and shared back office functions like enrollment, claims, pharmacy and quality reporting across several rural sites. Who pays: the PACE organization earns the combined Medicare and Medicaid monthly payment, and the partner takes a management fee or shares in the margin. Why now: a Democratic Congress and bipartisan interest in PACE expansion, rural health transformation money that states must spend, and rural hospitals looking for new roles as inpatient volumes fall. Risks: approval takes a long time, often a year or two; enrolling enough participants in sparse areas is hard; and taking full financial risk for frail people needs real capital, which is the lesson from the failures we discussed Friday. Some remote check ins could use simple home monitoring, but the core business is care coordination, staffing and transportation. One local note: in Pennsylvania, PACE is called LIFE, and from memory the state has one of the larger networks, so the real question is which rural counties are still left out. The first step: talk with the Pennsylvania Department of Aging and two or three rural hospitals in western Pennsylvania about whether any county is underserved by existing PACE programs, and what a small site would cost to launch.

A question to think about: if the hardest part of rural elder care is getting people and caregivers across long distances, which costs less over five years, moving the care to the person, or moving the person to the care? That's your briefing. Have a good Sunday.
