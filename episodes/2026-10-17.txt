Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Saturday, October seventeenth. It's a quieter weekend edition, so it's built around one big idea: where older people live, and what happens when the options fill up. Three things. Senior housing is nearly full, and almost nothing new is being built. Hospice companies are being bought and sold again, but buyers now care most about compliance. And a national study shows how much time people with dementia actually spend at home varies hugely from state to state. Today's learning segment is the senior living continuum, from staying at home to nursing homes.

First, senior housing. According to NIC MAP, the industry's main data source, average occupancy in senior housing reached about ninety percent in the second quarter of this year, and by the third quarter more than half of the big metro markets were at or above ninety percent. NIC has said for a while that occupancy would pass ninety percent nationally by the end of this year. The reason is simple: the baby boomers are arriving and construction collapsed after interest rates went up, so very few new buildings are opening. Why this matters to you: when buildings are full, prices rise and the people who get squeezed out are the middle class, who make too much for Medicaid but can't afford around six thousand dollars a month or more for assisted living. For most of them, home becomes the default whether it's ready or not. That's where today's opportunity comes from.

Second, hospice deals are back. Hospice News reports that after a three year slump, the M and A advisory firm The Braff Group projects about forty four hospice transactions this year, up from just twenty five in 2023. But what buyers want has changed. Compliance now comes first, ahead of growth. Medicare has put hospices in six states, California, Arizona, Nevada, Texas, Ohio and Georgia, under a period of enhanced oversight where claims are reviewed before they're paid, and audits and payment clawbacks are common. Advisors are telling sellers to do their own billing audit before going to market. Remember from Tuesday that Medicare has also frozen new hospice and home health enrollments nationally. Put those together and the value is in clean, existing licenses with good leaders. A new entrant would be better off buying a small, compliant agency than starting from scratch.

Third, a study in JAMA Network Open this summer measured days at home, meaning days not spent in a hospital, nursing home or other facility, for over half a million older adults with dementia who are on both Medicare and Medicaid. On average they spent about two hundred thirty eight days a year at home. Those using Medicaid home and community based services averaged about two hundred seventy five. But the state you live in made a huge difference: Kentucky averaged about one hundred fifty nine days, while Oregon averaged about two hundred eighty three and Alaska about three hundred twelve. Days at home is becoming a favorite outcome measure for programs and payers, because it's simple and it's what families actually care about. Any service you build in this space should be able to show its effect on days at home.

On business more broadly, the theme this week has been labor and capacity. Senior housing is full, nursing homes are shrinking, home care is short of workers, and hospices are frozen out of new licenses. Every one of those points to more care happening at home, organized by families.

On the social side, it was quiet, which is normal for a weekend. I couldn't reach Reddit or Hacker News directly.

The Resilient angle. The opportunities here are about housing, services and coordination, not devices. Three thoughts. First, the middle market, people with some savings but not enough for assisted living, is the largest underserved group, and they'll be aging at home by default. Second, in heavily regulated markets like hospice and home health, the asset is a clean license and good compliance systems, which suits a company used to quality systems and regulators, like Resilient. Third, days at home is the scoreboard. If you build a service, design it so you can measure that from day one.

Today's learning segment is the senior living continuum. Think of it as a ladder of increasing care. At the bottom is aging in place, living in your own home, sometimes with home care or adult day programs. Next is independent living, apartments for older adults with meals and activities but little or no care. Then assisted living, which adds help with daily activities like bathing, dressing and medications, typically for several thousand dollars a month, often in the range of five to seven thousand, and mostly paid out of pocket. That's an approximate figure; costs vary a lot by region. Memory care is a secured version of assisted living for people with dementia, and it costs more. At the top is the skilled nursing facility, or nursing home, with round the clock nursing, often over ten thousand dollars a month for a private room. Medicare covers short rehab stays there, and Medicaid pays for most long term residents. Continuing care retirement communities bundle all of these levels on one campus, usually with a large entrance fee. Two key facts. First, assisted living is overwhelmingly private pay, with only limited Medicaid waiver coverage in many states, which is why the middle class gets stuck. A widely cited NORC study projected that by around 2029, more than half of middle income seniors wouldn't be able to afford assisted living. That's from memory and worth checking. Second, most people never move up the ladder at all. The large majority of older adults live at home until the end, which is why home based services are the biggest market.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: continuing care at home for the middle market. Some continuing care communities already sell a membership instead of an apartment. Members stay in their own homes, pay an entrance fee and monthly dues, and get a care coordinator, home safety assessments and modifications, help finding and managing aides, and some coverage for care costs later, a bit like a mix of a concierge service and long term care insurance. The idea is to build that for middle income families in Pennsylvania who are priced out of full buildings: a lower cost membership focused on coordination, family caregiver training and support, and negotiated rates with vetted home care agencies, possibly tied to the paid family caregiver programs we discussed yesterday for members who qualify. It could be sold directly to families, offered through employers as a caregiving benefit, or partnered with a senior living operator that has a waiting list it can't serve. The risks are real: if you promise to pay for care later, you take on insurance risk and regulation, and families are wary of upfront fees. A safer first version is coordination only, with no insurance promise. The first test is to call two Pennsylvania continuing care communities and ask whether they run an at home program, what it costs, and how many people on their waiting lists they turn away.

A question to think about this weekend: if most people will age at home no matter what, what is the one service a family would pay for every month, without being asked twice? That's your briefing. Enjoy your weekend.
