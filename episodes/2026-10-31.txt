Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Saturday, October thirty first. Three things today. Medicare drug plan premiums are set to jump about fifty percent next year for people in stand alone plans, which makes this open enrollment season unusually important. States are restricting Medicaid home care in the name of fraud control, and a federal judge pushed back on part of the new Medicaid work rules. And a new study says popular AI chatbots often fail to adjust medication doses for age and kidney function, a warning for anyone building AI for older adults. Today's learning segment is voice AI and companion robots, like ElliQ and the robotic pets that New York hands out by the tens of thousands.

First, drug plan premiums. KFF Health News reports that the average monthly premium for stand alone Medicare drug plans is projected to rise from thirty four dollars to fifty one dollars in 2027, about fifty percent. About ten point four million people without low income help would pay more if they keep their current plan, including four point one million people in zero premium plans that won't be offered next year. Deductibles rise too, and more people will pay a percentage of the drug's price instead of a fixed copay. KFF points to CMS ending a premium subsidy pilot early this summer. Medicare Advantage drug premiums rise much less, under two dollars a month on average. CMS itself projected only a one dollar increase, and KFF says CMS didn't explain how. One Virginia enrollee whose premium was going from about forty dollars to one hundred twenty two found a comparable plan for nineteen dollars. Open enrollment runs through December seventh. Why this matters: switching plans saves real money this year, and most older adults don't shop. That's a lot of stranded value for anyone who helps seniors navigate Medicare honestly. It also nudges people toward Medicare Advantage, where drug premiums are lower.

Second, Medicaid home care access. NBC News reports that Ohio legislators are pressing Governor DeWine to lift a six month freeze on new Medicaid home care providers that he imposed in May as part of a fraud crackdown, after NBC found it has kept some disabled people from getting caregivers. It's the state version of the federal home health and hospice freeze we've been tracking. Separately, the Associated Press reports a federal judge in Boston ruled that states don't have to make people with serious medical conditions jump through as many hoops to prove they're exempt from the new Medicaid work requirement. He didn't strike down the eighty hours a month requirement itself. And Home Health Care News reports home based care mergers fell by about half in the third quarter, to eleven deals from twenty three, according to the advisory firm Mertz Taggart, with buyers digging harder into compliance history. Why this matters: governments are clamping down on new providers, so existing, clean, licensed agencies become more valuable, and so do the tools and services that prove compliance.

Third, AI and medication safety. Healthcare Dive reports that Doximity tested popular general purpose AI models released this year on more than eight thousand five hundred dosing questions across twenty two hundred drugs. About two percent of answers had a serious or potentially lethal error, and the most common mistake was failing to adjust the dose for age, kidney function or drug interactions, even when that information was in the question. Doximity sells a competing dosing tool, so take the exact numbers with that in mind. Also from KFF's briefing, a Pew Research survey found sixty percent of Americans are uncomfortable with AI helping their doctor make a diagnosis or treatment plan. Why this matters: older adults are exactly the people whose doses need adjusting. Any AI product for seniors that touches medications needs guardrails and a human check.

A few more items. Senior Housing News reports that senior living landlords are on a buying spree, with Welltower at about twenty one billion dollars of investment activity this year and Ventas at over five billion, and developers returning because buildings are filling up. Another Senior Housing News story says operators like Mather and HumanGood are reorganizing around healthspan, meaning years of healthy life. Mather offers residents a profile that measures grip strength, walking speed, heart rate variability, sleep and loneliness, with staff called healthspan navigators. And Centene, the insurer, said its Medicare business will break even a year early, while its headcount fell by almost six thousand people in one quarter.

On the social side, I couldn't reach Reddit or Hacker News directly, so nothing reliable to report.

The Resilient angle. Two things. Senior living operators now have money and are starting to measure residents' physical function over time. Measuring things like gait, sleep and heart rate variability is where a wearable can genuinely help, and operators are a buyer that pays directly without waiting on Medicare codes. Second, the companion devices in today's learning segment talk to people all day but cannot call 911 or detect a fall. That gap between companionship and safety is real, and it could be filled by a partnership rather than a new product.

Today's learning segment is voice AI and companion robots. There are three main types. First, tabletop social robots. The best known is ElliQ, made by Intuition Robotics, a small device with a screen and a lamp like head that moves. Unlike Alexa, it starts conversations on its own. It suggests activities, reminds people about medications and appointments, runs video calls with family, and has a family app. Intuition Robotics raised twenty five million dollars led by Toyota's Woven Capital. Second, robotic pets. Ageless Innovation, a Hasbro spinoff, makes Joy for All cats, dogs and a bird that clips to a walker. They purr, bark and respond to touch, and they're cheap. Older, more expensive robots like the Japanese PARO seal, which from memory is cleared by FDA as a medical device, are used mainly in dementia care. Third, and newest, phone based AI companions such as Meela, which raised three and a half million dollars led by Bain Capital, and Joy Calls. These need no device at all. The AI simply calls the older person on their own phone at set times, chats, and sends family a summary.

Who pays? The surprise is government. New York's State Office for the Aging has handed out more than thirty one thousand robotic pets since 2018, and close to nine hundred ElliQ robots. In March, Washington state began covering ElliQ as a smart care device for Medicaid members receiving long term care at home, through its Community First Choice program, which a news report called the first program of its kind. Texas gave its University of Texas Medical Branch five million dollars for aging technology, and UTMB just started an eight week home study of ElliQ for people over sixty five. Some Medicare Advantage plans and local aging agencies also buy them, which I'm stating from general knowledge. Consumers can buy them directly, but older adults rarely pay a monthly fee for companionship on their own.

Does it work? The honest answer is that the evidence is weak but encouraging. The headline numbers, like ninety five percent of users reporting less loneliness, come from Intuition Robotics and its state partners, based on user surveys with no comparison group. People who keep using a device tend to like it, so these surveys overstate the effect. Washington's pilot reported about sixty daily interactions per user, which shows engagement but not health outcomes. For robotic pets, small studies show reductions in loneliness and agitation in dementia, but few large controlled trials exist. I'm not aware of any study yet showing these products reduce hospital visits or nursing home moves. The UTMB study, and the clinical trials that are starting, will help.

The risks are real. People with dementia may not understand they're talking to a machine. Constant listening raises privacy questions. A companion could replace human visits rather than add to them, especially when states are short of aides. And as today's dosing study shows, AI that offers health advice can be wrong in ways that matter more for older bodies. Also, ElliQ is not a medical alert device and can't call 911. Big tech has struggled here too. Amazon shut down its Alexa Together service for caregivers in 2024, which I'm citing from memory. The lesson is that a companion has to be sold to someone who pays for outcomes, such as a Medicaid program, a health plan or an aging agency, and it has to show more than satisfaction.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: an AI check-in call service for Medicaid home care programs. The customer is a state Medicaid long term care program, a Medicaid managed care plan, or an Area Agency on Aging. The problem: people receiving home care often see an aide for a few hours a week and are alone the rest of the time, while states are freezing new providers and short on workers. The service places a friendly AI phone call once or twice a day to the person's own phone, landline or cell, asks how they're doing, checks on meals, medications and mood, and sends anything concerning to a human care manager the same day. No device, no WiFi, no setup. Who pays: the state or health plan, as a covered service like Washington's, or as part of care coordination. Why now: Washington has shown states will pay for AI companionship, voice AI has become cheap and natural, and home care workforces are shrinking. Risks: the evidence bar, privacy, AI saying something wrong about medications, which today's study shows is a real danger, so the AI should never give dosing advice, and competition from Meela, Joy Calls and others. The edge would be selling to the payer with outcome data, not to families. No hardware is needed, though people at high fall risk could be offered automatic detection as an add on. A first test: run a ninety day pilot with one Pennsylvania Area Agency on Aging, perhaps in Allegheny County, and measure how many problems the calls catch that the care manager didn't know about.

A question for your weekend: if a state would pay for an AI to call a lonely older adult every day, what would that AI have to prove after six months for the state to keep paying? That's your briefing. Happy Halloween.
