Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Sunday, November first. It's a quieter news day, so this one leans toward the week ahead and a longer learning segment. Three things. Home health trade press is mapping out what Medicare might do when the freeze on new home health agencies expires in twelve days. Medicare is moving to put every home health agency in California under extra claims review, citing fraud. And this week brings Tuesday's midterm elections and, any day now, the final Medicare payment rules for doctors and home health, including the remote monitoring changes we've been tracking. Today's learning segment is home robots for physical tasks: what exists, what it costs, and what actually works for older adults.

First, the freeze. Home Health Care News published an analysis of the options as the national moratorium on new home health and hospice enrollment approaches November thirteenth. Medicare can extend it, which by law means another six months, let it expire, limit it to certain regions, or exempt established providers with clean compliance records, the gold card idea. The author expects a modified freeze rather than a blanket extension. Two useful points from the industry: the two hundred fifty blocked agencies are applications that weren't approved, not proven fraud, and a freeze does nothing about bad actors already enrolled. Also pending is the final home health payment rule for next year, which usually comes out around Halloween. In July, Medicare proposed a two point four percent overall increase, offset by a temporary three percent cut to the base rate. Why this matters: whichever way this goes, Medicare is building tools that reward agencies with clean records, and that shapes who can grow in home health next year.

Second, California. Home Health Care News reports Medicare filed an emergency request to expand the home health Review Choice Demonstration to California, citing significant fraud in the state. That program already covers Illinois, Ohio, Oklahoma, North Carolina, Florida and Texas. Agencies in it must choose to have claims reviewed before or after payment, and those that get ninety percent of their claims approved over six months earn relief from most reviews. The notice is due to be published tomorrow with a fourteen day comment period. AccentCare, one of the largest agencies, has said it supports expanding the program nationwide. Why this matters: California is one of the biggest home health markets, and every agency there will need cleaner documentation, fast. Combined with the freeze, this is the clearest sign yet that Medicare is managing home health through compliance rather than payment cuts alone.

Third, the week ahead. Tuesday's midterms decide several governors' races in states with Medicaid budget gaps, like Oregon, and control of Congress, which affects Medicaid funding and the PACE expansion bill we discussed this week. The final physician payment rule for next year is due around now. It will settle whether Medicare stops paying for remote patient monitoring run by outside vendors, whether patients need a face to face visit first, and how the billing codes get consolidated. Medicare open enrollment continues through December seventh, with stand alone drug plan premiums projected to rise about fifty percent. I'll cover each rule as soon as it publishes.

The Resilient angle. Today's robotics segment has a clear lesson for any hardware company in this market: the robots that are actually getting used by older adults are simple, single purpose machines sold to care organizations, not general humanoids sold to families. Resilient's own path looks similar, institutional buyers first, with a narrow job done reliably. Also, home robots that move around the house and watch over people raise the same safety question as companion robots: none of them is designed to detect a fall and summon help. That's a partnership opening, not a new product.

Today's learning segment is home robots for physical tasks. Start with the problem. Most of what keeps an older person out of a nursing home is physical help with daily activities: getting out of bed, bathing, toileting, dressing, moving around, carrying things, cooking and cleaning. Those tasks are hard for robots because homes are cluttered and unpredictable, and the tasks involve touching a fragile human body. So the field splits into three groups.

The first group is general purpose humanoids. The best known is NEO from 1X Technologies, a soft, lightweight humanoid that the company says will do household chores. It costs twenty thousand dollars, or four hundred ninety nine dollars a month on subscription. Preorders opened in October 2025, and 1X says its first year of production sold out within five days. It opened a factory in Hayward, California in the spring with capacity for ten thousand units in the first year. Deliveries were promised for this year, but I couldn't confirm how many have reached homes. The important detail is that for tasks NEO hasn't learned, a human expert at 1X can remotely control the robot during scheduled sessions, which means a stranger may be seeing inside your home. Today's humanoids are partly autonomous and partly teleoperated, and complex chores like laundry are not yet reliable. Other humanoid companies, like Figure and Tesla, talk about homes too, but from what I know they're mostly working in factories for now.

The second group is narrow assistive robots, which are more practical today. Labrador Systems makes the Retriever, essentially a smart rolling cart with a shelf that rises and lowers. It carries meals, laundry and heavy items around the house and brings things within reach for someone with pain or limited mobility. Its customers are mainly care providers, including On Lok, a PACE program in San Francisco, and senior living operators in California. A consumer version is listed at about fifteen hundred dollars plus one hundred dollars a month. Hello Robot's Stretch, a lightweight mobile arm, is used mostly in research and by some people with severe disabilities, which I'm citing from memory. Amazon's Astro home robot, priced at sixteen hundred dollars, patrols and video monitors but can't really carry anything, and it's still invitation only.

The third group is robots in care facilities, which is where Japan has led. From memory, Japan's government has funded robots for lifting and transferring patients, powered suits that help aides lift, walking assistance robots, and monitoring sensors under mattresses. A study described this spring in The Conversation found that many demonstrations happened in controlled settings, some robots were partly remote controlled, and real world adoption lags because care homes are busy and unpredictable. Researchers expect lifting aids, mobility aids and routine monitoring to spread first. From memory, research on Japanese nursing homes has found that monitoring sensors were adopted far more widely than physical care robots, and that facilities using robots did not cut staff, which is a useful counter to the idea that robots replace aides.

So who pays? Today, mostly care organizations, PACE programs and senior living operators, plus wealthy early adopters. Medicare doesn't cover home robots. Some could qualify as durable medical equipment if they're medically necessary and approved, but none has a clear benefit category yet. Medicaid waiver programs sometimes pay for assistive technology, and Medicare Advantage plans can offer it as a supplemental benefit for chronically ill members. Economically, the comparison is a home care aide, which runs roughly thirty to forty dollars an hour, from memory. A five hundred dollar a month robot only has to save about four aide hours a week to pay for itself, but only if it reliably does something that aides would otherwise do.

My take: in the next three years, useful home robots for older adults will be simple carriers, lifting aids and monitoring, sold to care organizations that can measure the results. General humanoids caring for frail people at home are further out, and the first versions will depend on remote human operators, which is really a new kind of staffing model with a robot body.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: an assistive robot deployment service for PACE programs and senior living. The customer is a PACE program, an assisted living operator or a home care agency. The problem: robot makers are small and focused on engineering, while care organizations don't know which robots work, how to train staff, or how to prove value, so most pilots stall. The service would be vendor neutral. It picks the right robot for each job, such as fetch and carry, lifting aids or night monitoring, leases it, installs it, trains staff and residents, maintains it, and measures outcomes like aide hours saved, falls and resident satisfaction, then reports them to the operator. Who pays: the operator, through a monthly fee per robot that includes everything. Why now: humanoid makers have big funding and factories coming online, simpler robots already work, and care organizations face a worker shortage. Risks: robots may not save enough staff time to justify the fee, vendors may fail, and liability if a robot hurts someone. Hardware is involved, but it's other companies' hardware; the business is the service and the outcome data. The first test is a ninety day pilot with one Pittsburgh area PACE program or senior living operator, deploying three fetch and carry robots and measuring aide minutes saved per day.

A question to think about: if a humanoid robot in an older person's home needs a remote human to help it for the hard tasks, who should that human be, and is that a robot company or a home care agency? That's your briefing. Have a good Sunday.
