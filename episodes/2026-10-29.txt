Today's learning segment is ambient and passive sensing compared with wearables. Ambient sensing means sensors placed in the home or room rather than on the body. The simplest are motion sensors, door and contact sensors, bed and chair pressure pads, and smart plugs that show whether the kettle or TV gets used. Newer ones include radar sensors on the wall that can see posture, movement, breathing and falls through walls without a camera, Wi Fi sensing that reads changes in wireless signals, and AI cameras that detect falls and record what happened. The big advantage over wearables is that nobody has to remember to wear or charge anything, which, as we heard yesterday, is the main reason wearables get abandoned. Radar and Wi Fi also avoid cameras, which matters for privacy, especially in bathrooms where many falls happen. The disadvantages: sensors only work in the rooms where they're installed, they struggle when two people or pets share a space, installation and coverage take effort, and most consumer products haven't published independent accuracy data. They also measure behavior and location, not physiology. A wearable can tell you heart rate or oxygen; a radar sensor mostly tells you where someone is, how they move, whether they fell, and sometimes breathing rate.

What's the evidence? The longest running research is from the University of Missouri's TigerPlace, where motion, radar and depth sensors in apartments measured walking speed and stride, and their research suggested changes in sensor patterns could flag health problems days or weeks before clinical events. That's from memory, so treat the details as approximate. In memory care, SafelyYou's AI camera system had a pilot published in the American Journal of Managed Care: residents with dementia using the system had far fewer emergency department visits after falls than a control group, though the study was small, the authors had ties to the company, and some of the headline eighty percent reduction reflects fewer falls overall. On the consumer side, products like Vayyar's wall mounted radar, around two hundred fifty dollars, and Gamgee's Wi Fi fall detection kit, around three hundred twenty dollars, claim high accuracy without published independent tests.

So where does each approach win? Ambient sensing is strongest for fall detection and daily routine monitoring in a controlled setting like assisted living or memory care, where the operator pays, installs and responds, and in homes of people who won't wear anything, especially those with dementia. Wearables win when you need physiology, like heart rhythm, oxygen or blood pressure, or when the person moves around outside the home. The best programs often combine them, and as always, the value comes from a person who responds to what the sensors show. The business lesson from the past few years: selling ambient systems to senior living operators, as companies like SafelyYou and Inspiren do, has worked better than selling gadgets to families, because the operator has the staff, the liability and the budget.
