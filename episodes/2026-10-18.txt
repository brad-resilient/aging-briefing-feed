Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Sunday, October eighteenth. Three things today. Wall Street is paying billions for clinics that care for older patients, as Humana swaps its private equity partner for BlackRock. A large trial shows that a simple, no hardware nudge in the doctor's office cut falls by about nine percent. And a new survey of family caregivers shows how much the job is costing them in work and money. Today's learning segment is home care economics: how agencies actually make money, and why so many struggle.

First, the money. On Tuesday, Humana announced a joint venture with HPS, part of BlackRock, to own and run its CenterWell senior primary care clinics. That's about one hundred fifty clinics built around Medicare Advantage patients. BlackRock is buying out the previous private equity owner, Welsh Carson, for about two point eight billion dollars. Welsh Carson first invested in 2020 and put in at least one point eight billion dollars, so this is a normal private equity exit, not a fire sale. Humana says the deal gives it cheaper capital, and the new partners have about two years to put money into existing clinics, new locations and acquisitions. Humana can start buying clinics back in 2028. Why this matters: even in a year when Medicare Advantage plans are cutting back, big investors still want clinics that manage the whole cost of an older patient's care. The value isn't the office visit. It's the ability to keep people out of the hospital.

There's a home based version of the same idea. Home Health Care News reports that Ennoble Care, a New Jersey company that sends primary care clinicians to older patients' homes, entered five new states in September, including Ohio and Kentucky, and now serves about fifty thousand patients in sixteen states. It wants to add about ten more states in the next year. Ennoble says roughly seven million Americans need home based primary care but only about one in seven gets it, and that hospitalizations fall by more than half for patients who stay with it for over two years. That last number is the company's own claim, so treat it carefully.

Second, falls. A cluster trial called eSTEPS, published Friday in JAMA Network Open, tested something very simple. In twenty two primary care practices at Mass General Brigham, the electronic health record showed clinicians a quiet alert and a one click order set to prescribe home strength and balance exercises, a simplified version of the Otago program, about ten minutes a session, starting with seated or beginner levels. Patients got handouts, portal messages, a website and an app. The study followed almost twelve thousand adults aged sixty five and older who had screened positive for fall risk. Falls were about nineteen per hundred patient years in the practices with the tool, versus about twenty one in usual care, roughly nine percent fewer. About twenty nine patients had to be covered for two years to prevent one fall. That's a modest effect, but it cost almost nothing per patient and needed no devices at all. The lesson for anyone in falls: the bar for a paid falls product is now "better than a free nudge in the health record," and the real gap is getting people to actually do the exercises.

Third, family caregivers. A survey of eleven hundred forty four family caregivers by Aeroflow Urology, a medical supply company, so read it as marketing research rather than science, found that fifty seven percent had cut their work hours and eleven percent had quit their jobs to provide care. Seventy seven percent said they feel financially overwhelmed. Nearly half do hands on tasks like bathing and helping someone move with no training, and close to nine in ten wish their doctors gave them more help and resources. These numbers line up with the bigger national surveys we covered on Thursday. The message keeps repeating: caregivers are losing income, doing clinical work untrained, and not getting help from the health system.

On the business and policy side, the national freeze on new hospice and home health agencies in Medicare is set to expire on November thirteenth. As of this week, CMS hadn't said whether it will be extended. Lawyers quoted by Home Health Care News say the freeze has also blocked some ownership changes, so small owners hoping to sell are stuck, and deals have stalled. If it ends, expect a rush of new applications and pent up acquisitions. If it's extended, existing licenses get even more valuable. We'll watch for the announcement.

One more story, from Maine, that's a nice reality check on technology. The Portland Press Herald reports that a program called Lifelong Maine has put six hundred six free Amazon Echo Dots into older adults' homes, paid for by a thirty five thousand dollar university grant. Seventy two percent of recipients were comfortable using them within two months. The state's assistive technology lending library nearly doubled its loans in two years, and it now lends an ElliQ companion robot. The lesson: cheap consumer tech plus a little training goes a long way, and the training is the part nobody pays for.

On the social side, it was a quiet weekend. I couldn't reach Reddit or Hacker News directly.

The Resilient angle. Three thoughts, and only one involves hardware. First, the money is following organizations that take responsibility for the total cost of care, like CenterWell and home based primary care groups. Those are the buyers to understand, whatever Resilient sells. Second, the falls trial raises the bar for any falls product, including a wearable: it has to beat a nearly free software nudge, and it has to show fewer falls, not just better detection. Third, the hardest operating problem in this whole market is keeping caregivers, which is a people and process problem, not a device problem.

Today's learning segment is home care economics. There are three very different businesses that all get called home care. The first is Medicare skilled home health: nurses and therapists after a hospital stay, paid by Medicare per thirty day period. It's clinical, heavily regulated, and right now frozen to new entrants. The second is Medicaid personal care: aides who help with bathing, dressing and meals, paid by state Medicaid programs or their managed care plans at a fixed hourly rate the state sets. The third is private duty, the same kind of help paid out of pocket by families, often through franchise brands. Here's how the math works for the private duty and Medicaid side. An agency bills a client or a plan by the hour, often somewhere in the thirties per hour for private pay, and pays the caregiver around twenty dollars an hour. Those rates are approximate and vary a lot by state, so check them before using them. After caregiver wages and payroll taxes, gross margin is often around thirty to forty percent, and after recruiting, scheduling, insurance, office staff and, for franchises, royalty fees, net profit is often in the single digits. That's an inference from industry rules of thumb, not a single source. The killer is turnover. The industry benchmark firm Activated Insights reported that home based care turnover was about seventy nine percent a year, nearly four out of five caregivers, and estimates each departure costs an agency about twenty six hundred dollars to replace. Most of those departures happen in the first few months. The other big risk is depending on one payer. In September, 24 Hour Home Care, one of California's largest agencies, announced it would lay off seven hundred thirty eight workers after the health plan Health Net decided to end a personal care program that funded their work. One contract decision, hundreds of jobs gone. Two last facts. Small agencies typically sell for a few times the owner's annual earnings, while large platforms sell for much higher multiples, which is why private equity rolls them up. And the agencies that win tend to have three things: steady hours for caregivers, a mix of payers, and fast, reliable response to new client inquiries. Activated Insights found agencies that track every inquiry had median revenue of about three point one five million dollars, more than double those that don't.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: caregiver retention as a service for home care agencies. The problem is turnover near eighty percent, with each departure costing about twenty six hundred dollars, and most people leaving in their first ninety days. The idea is a service that takes over the first ninety days for an agency's new caregivers: a structured onboarding, a peer mentor they can call, guaranteed minimum hours through smart scheduling across clients, quick skills training for the hands on tasks that scare new aides, and a weekly check in that flags who is about to quit. It could be priced per caregiver still employed at ninety days, so the agency only pays when it works. Who pays: agencies directly, or Medicaid managed care plans that can't fill authorized hours, and perhaps the paid family caregiver agencies we discussed on Friday. Why now: shortages are worse, immigration is tightening the supply of workers, and the moratorium makes each existing agency's workforce more valuable. Risks: agencies run on thin margins and are slow to buy, software firms already sell retention surveys, and you'd need proof fast. Hardware is not needed at all. Resilient's fit is its Pittsburgh base, a good place to pilot with local agencies, and its experience running structured clinical programs. The first test is to ask three Pittsburgh agencies how many caregivers they hired last year, how many were still there at ninety days, and what they'd pay to keep one more.

A question to think about this Sunday: in a business where four out of five workers leave every year, is the real product care for older adults, or a better job for the people who give it? That's your briefing. Have a good Sunday.
