Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Friday, November sixth. Three things today. The home care industry is sizing up a Democratic Congress, and the idea getting the most attention is a universal home care benefit based on need rather than age or income. Addus HomeCare, one of the biggest personal care companies, says its caregivers want full forty hour weeks, so short visits are going unstaffed. And the federal rural health fund, fifty billion dollars over five years, has missed its deadline to tell states how much they get next year. Today's learning segment is notable failures in aging tech and what they teach. And there's a business idea at the end that comes straight out of the Addus story.

First, home care after the election. The latest Associated Press count, as reported by KFF Health News on Wednesday afternoon, has Democrats with at least two hundred thirty seven House seats and at least fifty four Senate seats, with Alaska and Mississippi still unresolved. Home Health Care News asked industry groups what this means. The main policy anchor is a request for information that Senate Finance Committee Democrats sent out in October about home care and long term care. It asks about the Medicaid access rule, including the rule that eighty percent of Medicaid home care payments go to worker pay, and it floats a universal home care benefit based on functional need, not age or income. The Home Care Association of America calls a Democratic Congress, quote, a whole new, different set of opportunities. Its vice president of government relations, Eric Reinarman, said the big challenge is truly addressing the workforce crisis, because that's the rate limiting factor. The association is pushing for visa reform, including a new visa category for home care workers. Denis Fleming of the National Alliance for Care at Home was more measured. He told providers to expect a different tone, but not a change in priorities, noting that CMS still sets Medicare home health and hospice rates each year through its own rules. And remember, President Trump still has the veto, so any large new benefit is a long shot before 2029. My read: the hearings and bill drafts over the next two years will shape the next big long term care fight, and it's worth tracking who gets invited to testify.

Providers also weighed in on the home health rule we covered yesterday. Home Health Care News reports the two point four percent raise, about four hundred fifteen million dollars, is the biggest update in nearly two decades outside of 2022. But Dan Savitt, who runs VNS Health in New York, points out that Medicare home health payments have risen only about five point eight percent in total since 2020, compared with twenty percent or more for hospitals and nursing homes. Stacie Bratcher, chief executive of LiveWell Partners, said a two point four percent update doesn't keep pace with what agencies pay to recruit and retain clinical staff. Steven Gonzalez, who runs HealthView Home Healthcare Services, said, quote, I'm glad the tools are pointed at the bad actors. One more detail from the rule: when a home health agency, hospice or medical equipment supplier changes owners, the new owner now has to enroll as a new provider and go through a survey or accreditation. That makes buying agencies slower and riskier, which matters for anyone thinking about growing by acquisition.

Second, Addus and the forty hour week. Addus HomeCare serves about sixty two thousand people in twenty four states, mostly through Medicaid funded personal care, which means help with bathing, dressing and meals rather than nursing. Revenue last quarter was three hundred eighty eight million dollars, up seven percent. But the number of people it served in personal care went down. Chief executive Dirk Allison explained it this way, in Home Health Care News's paraphrase: caregivers want forty hour weeks, so cases that need only a few hours a week are hard to staff. Allison said the company is focusing on patients who give it more hours. Addus is also buying AccentCare's personal care business for about two hundred seventy five million dollars, adding about thirteen thousand clients in ten states, expected to close early next year. And it's rolling out a caregiver app called Addus Connect.

Why this matters. The people who need only a few hours a week are often the ones at the earliest stage of decline: an older adult who needs help with a shower three times a week, or someone recently home from the hospital. If agencies can't staff them, those people go without help until a fall or a crisis, and then they need much more care. This is a structural problem, not just an Addus problem. Short visits mean unpaid drive time and split shifts for workers, so workers prefer long cases, and agencies follow the workers.

Third, rural health money. KFF Health News reports that CMS missed its October thirty first deadline to tell states their share of next year's Rural Health Transformation Program, the fifty billion dollar fund created by last year's budget law. An email the day before cited, quote, an additional administrative process requirement. Each state's base amount of one hundred million dollars is still available, but the competitive portion, which varied widely last year, has no announcement date. In 2026, states got between one hundred forty seven million and two hundred eighty one million dollars. KFF's review found that states with Republican governors tended to get more of the competitive money, and six states reported consulting contracts adding up to more than sixty million dollars. For Pennsylvania, which has a large rural older population, the delay makes it harder to plan programs like rural home monitoring or community paramedics that this money could pay for.

A few more items. North Carolina Health News reports about forty thousand North Carolinians will lose their current Medicare plans at the end of the year, part of more than four million people nationally whose Medicare Advantage or drug plans are being discontinued for 2027, according to KFF. The average standalone drug plan premium is projected to rise from thirty four to fifty one dollars a month, and the yearly out of pocket cap for drugs rises from twenty one hundred to twenty four hundred dollars. Open enrollment ends December seventh. CVS reported strong quarterly earnings, with net income of two point one seven billion dollars, helped by Aetna's Medicare business, according to Modern Healthcare. And The Washington Post ran a piece on six steps for people aging solo, without children or a spouse to help, which is a fast growing group worth keeping in mind.

Now the learning segment: notable failures in aging tech and what they teach. Most of what follows is from memory, so treat the specifics as approximate. A few patterns stand out.

First, retail giants that bought into senior care and backed out. Best Buy bought GreatCall, the maker of Jitterbug phones and medical alert devices, for about eight hundred million dollars in 2018, and later bought Current Health, a remote monitoring company. A few years later it sold Current Health back to its founder and pulled back from the health business. Amazon shut down Amazon Care, its virtual and in home primary care service, at the end of 2022, saying it wasn't a complete enough offering for large employers. Walmart closed all of its health clinics in 2024, saying it couldn't make the model profitable. The lesson: a famous brand and lots of customers don't solve the hard parts of care, which are clinical staffing, insurance contracts and slow sales to health systems and plans.

Second, companies that took on financial risk for older patients and got crushed. Babylon Health and Cano Health both built businesses around Medicare Advantage patients, and both collapsed, Babylon in 2023 and Cano into bankruptcy in 2024. When medical costs rose and Medicare's payment formulas tightened, the losses came fast. The lesson: taking on full cost risk for older adults is a business of insurance math, and a thin cushion of cash can't absorb a bad year.

Third, products nobody would pay for. Pear Therapeutics got FDA clearance for prescription apps but went bankrupt in 2023 because insurers mostly wouldn't cover them. Jibo, a friendly social robot, shut down within a couple of years. Many medical alert pendants end up in drawers because older adults don't want to look frail. The lessons: regulatory clearance is not a payment model, and the person who pays, the person who buys and the person who uses the product are often three different people, an insurer, an adult child and an older parent. A product has to work for all three.

What tends to work instead? Companies that attach to an existing payment stream, like Medicare billing codes or Medicaid waivers, that keep human service in the loop, and that measure something a payer already cares about, such as hospital admissions or falls. That's a useful filter for every idea in this briefing, including the next one.

Today's opportunity, and it's not a device. A neighborhood care pod service that solves the short visit problem Addus described. The idea: group older adults who each need a few hours of personal care a week into small geographic clusters, a few blocks or one senior apartment building, and build one full time, forty hour route for a single caregiver who serves five to eight clients in that cluster. Software handles the matching and scheduling, but the real work is operations: recruiting clients in dense areas, coordinating with Medicaid care managers to schedule visits that fit the route, and keeping continuity so each client sees the same caregiver. Who pays: Medicaid managed care plans and state waiver programs through the normal personal care rates, or agencies that license the model to fill hours they now can't staff. Senior housing operators with affordable buildings could also host pods. Why now: Addus says short cases go unstaffed, workforce is the industry's rate limiting factor, and a Democratic Congress is focused on home care access. Risks: Medicaid rules on billing and authorized hours vary by state, clients have different needs and schedules, and clusters can fall apart when clients move or pass away. Nothing like this needs hardware. The first test: pick one dense Pittsburgh neighborhood or a large senior apartment building, find six to eight people with short authorized hours who are currently going without care, and see whether one caregiver can serve them reliably on a forty hour week for three months.

A question to think about: if workers want forty hour weeks and many older adults need only four, is the real fix a scheduling trick, or a change in how Medicaid pays for care, for example paying per building or per neighborhood rather than per visit? That's your briefing. Have a good weekend.
