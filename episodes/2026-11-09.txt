First, the freeze. Yesterday I said I couldn't confirm the November thirteenth date. Today I read the notice in the Federal Register. It says the moratorium on new Medicare enrollment of hospices took effect May thirteenth, 2026, and lasts six months. It can be extended in six month steps, and any extension has to be published as a new notice. A matching freeze covers new home health agencies, according to Healthcare Dive's May coverage. So the original November thirteenth date stands, and I apologize for the back and forth. As of Friday's reporting, I found no extension notice. CMS gave its reasons in the notice: hospice enrollment more than doubled between 2019 and 2023 in Arizona, California and Nevada, with schemes where companies open, bill, and close quickly, and in an enhanced review of six hundred seventy hospices, about eighteen percent had their enrollment revoked, compared with a typical one to three percent. When the freeze was announced, Doctor Oz said, quote, today we're shutting the door on fraud. Home Health Care News has reported that about two hundred fifty home health agencies were blocked. What to watch this week: a Federal Register notice extending the freeze, a modified version limited to certain states, or a quiet lapse. An industry voice quoted by Home Health Care News made the key point: a freeze stops new entrants, but by itself it does not remove fraudulent providers already in the program.

Second, private equity and hospice. Healthcare Dive reports on a study in Health Affairs that looked at Medicare claims from 2016 to 2022. It compared one hundred ninety one for profit hospices bought by private equity firms with about thirteen hundred for profit hospices that weren't. After a private equity purchase, Medicare spending rose about one thousand dollars more per hospice stay than at the comparison hospices. Patients stayed about six days longer on average, more patients were discharged alive, and the share of patients with dementia went up. The researchers wrote that this may represent an increase in potentially inappropriate or inappropriately early admissions to hospice. According to the article, private equity owned about fifteen percent of hospices in 2026, up from three percent in 2012. The researchers suggested paying hospices a daily rate adjusted for how sick patients are, the way hospitals are paid. This connects to the hospice economics we covered last week: because hospice is paid a flat daily rate, longer stays with stable patients are the most profitable. Expect this study to show up in hearings in the new Congress.

Third, the CMS paperwork rule. Skilled Nursing News reports that CMS proposed a rule it calls Cutting Administrative Requirements for Excellence in Patient Care, or CARE. After reviewing twenty one types of providers, CMS estimates the rule would save three hundred twenty million dollars and more than three million staff hours. For nursing homes, it would end three Medicaid payment transparency reports, end weekly reporting left over from COVID, let residents opt out of having discharge notices sent to the state long term care ombudsman, and ask for comment on letting physician assistants and nurse practitioners do more in nursing homes. The nursing home trade group AHCA welcomed what it called a smarter oversight system. The transparency reports are the controversial part, because advocates and even LeadingAge have raised concerns about owners moving money away from care through related companies. A LeadingAge official called that kind of tunneling of funds, quote, reprehensible. With Democrats taking Congress, expect pushback on dropping those reports.

Also worth a quick mention. Humana said early sign ups for Medicare Advantage next year are stronger than expected, even after dropping plans that covered about six hundred thousand members. The share of its members in plans rated four stars or higher jumps from forty one percent this year to ninety three percent next year, which means higher bonus payments. And a reminder of the stakes in Medicare Advantage billing: KFF Health News reported this summer that Elevance paid the government three hundred forty two million dollars amid a CMS probe into alleged overcharging, after Kaiser Permanente paid five hundred fifty six million in a fraud settlement in January.

Now the learning segment: opioids, pain management and overdose risk in older adults. Three things to understand.

First, the overdose numbers are rising fast in older people, from a low base. A study presented at the American Society of Anesthesiologists meeting last year used CDC death records and found fentanyl related deaths among adults sixty five and older rose from two hundred sixty four in 2015 to more than four thousand in 2023. And about half of those deaths in 2023 also involved a stimulant, usually cocaine or methamphetamine, up from under nine percent in 2015. Many of these are people who have used drugs for decades and are now older, not just new users. So addiction care, including methadone and buprenorphine treatment, is now an elder care issue too. From memory, Medicare has covered opioid treatment programs since 2020.

Second, prescribed medications carry their own risks in older bodies. This part is from memory and general clinical guidance. Older adults clear drugs more slowly, are more sensitive to sedation, and often take several drugs that interact. The riskiest combination is an opioid with another sedating drug, like a benzodiazepine for anxiety or sleep, gabapentin or pregabalin for nerve pain, or certain sleep aids and muscle relaxants. Together they can slow breathing, especially during sleep, and the risk is higher in people with sleep apnea, lung disease or kidney problems. That's respiratory depression, the next topic on our curriculum. The American Geriatrics Society's Beers Criteria, a widely used list of drugs to avoid or use carefully in older adults, specifically warns against combining opioids with benzodiazepines or gabapentinoids. Opioids and sedatives also raise the risk of falls and confusion.

Third, undertreated pain is also a problem. Chronic pain is common in older adults, and cutting off opioids abruptly can cause withdrawal, uncontrolled pain and even push people toward illicit drugs. The safer path is careful tapering, non drug treatments like physical therapy, and making the naloxone overdose reversal drug available at home. Medicare drug plans already run opioid safety edits at the pharmacy, flagging high doses and risky combinations, from memory.

What does this mean for Resilient? This is my analysis. The people most at risk of breathing problems at night, older adults on opioids plus another sedating drug, often with sleep apnea, are a well defined group that health plans can find in pharmacy claims. That's a clear target population for monitoring products, but the business case gets much stronger when monitoring is paired with something that lowers the risk itself, like a medication review.

Today's opportunity, and it's mostly a service, with hardware optional. A pharmacist led sedative safety program for Medicare Advantage plans, accountable care organizations and senior living. The program uses pharmacy claims to find older members taking an opioid together with a benzodiazepine, gabapentinoid or other sedative. A clinical pharmacist reviews each person's medications, works with the prescribing doctor on a gradual taper or a safer alternative, screens for sleep apnea, makes sure naloxone is in the home, and teaches family members what an overdose looks like. For the highest risk people, overnight breathing or oxygen monitoring could be added for the taper period, which is a natural place for a wearable, but the core value is the medication review. Who pays: plans and accountable care organizations, per member reviewed or per member per month, because these drug combinations drive falls, emergency visits and hospital stays, and plans are already measured by Medicare on opioid safety. Why now: overdose deaths in older adults are climbing, accountable care organizations just got richer incentives in last week's payment rule, and the payment rule also created coaching and care management pathways. Risks: doctors may resist changing prescriptions they've managed for years, tapers have to be slow and individualized to avoid harm, and plans may already run some version of this through their pharmacy benefit managers. The first test: work with one Pittsburgh area plan, accountable care organization or senior living operator, review one hundred members on risky combinations, and measure how many reduce or stop the combination over six months, plus falls and emergency visits.

A question to think about: if half of overdose deaths in older adults now involve stimulants as well as fentanyl, are the right partners for older adult overdose prevention the geriatric clinics, or the addiction treatment programs that most older adults never walk into?
