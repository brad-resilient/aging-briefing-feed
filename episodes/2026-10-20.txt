Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Tuesday, October twentieth. Three things today. Medicare is going after so called ghost networks, the provider lists in Medicare Advantage plans that are full of doctors who aren't really available. A Johns Hopkins study links frequent daytime napping to a higher risk of dementia. And senior living keeps consolidating, while one of the big names in home based acute care is pulling back. Today's learning segment is Medicare's GUIDE model for dementia care, what it pays for and who's using it.

First, ghost networks. On Friday, CMS proposed a rule that would require Medicare Advantage plans to check and update their provider directories at least every ninety days, starting in 2028. If a directory wrongly lists a doctor as in network, the member would only pay in network cost sharing. Starting in 2029, plans would have to show accuracy scores prominently, and CMS would publish them. The same rule would stop pharmacy benefit managers from tying their Part D pay to drug list prices, and would drop generic drug copays to zero for the lowest income people on both Medicare and Medicaid, starting January first. Separately, NBC reports that some stand alone Part D drug plans will rise fifty dollars or more a month next year, at least six hundred dollars a year for people who don't switch. Why this matters: directory accuracy, network adequacy and open enrollment shopping are all becoming measured and enforced. That creates demand for data services that keep directories accurate, and it nudges plans to prove members can actually get care, especially in home care and behavioral health, where networks are thinnest.

Second, napping and dementia. Researchers at Johns Hopkins followed nine hundred ninety five Medicare beneficiaries aged sixty five and older, from the National Health and Aging Trends Study, for up to ten years. People who said they napped most or all days had about a forty eight percent higher risk of developing dementia than people who rarely napped, and people whose naps were unintentional, dozing off without meaning to, had about a thirty nine percent higher risk. Long naps over thirty minutes weren't significantly linked after accounting for nighttime sleep. The study was published in the Journal of Gerontology. Two big caveats: it's an association, not proof that napping causes dementia, and napping was self reported. The authors think frequent, unplanned napping may be an early sign of brain changes, and they call for studies using wearable sleep tracking. For you, that's one of the rare cases today where a device is genuinely relevant: daytime sleep patterns are something passive sensors can measure better than a questionnaire. But it's an early research signal, not a product market yet.

Third, the business of where people live and get care. Discovery Senior Living agreed on Monday to acquire Sunshine Retirement Living, an Oregon based operator managing thirty three communities with more than three thousand five hundred units, mostly independent living, plus some assisted living and memory care. The buildings stay with their owners, including the real estate investment trust Sabra, and Discovery takes over the management company. This is the pattern in senior housing: operators consolidate to get scale while landlords own the real estate, and with occupancy near ninety percent, as we covered on Saturday, everyone wants more beds under management. On the care side, Modern Healthcare reports that DispatchHealth, which merged with Medically Home last year to become one of the largest hospital at home providers, has scaled back clinical services in its emergency department alternative program with six health system partners, including job cuts. That came days after the company announced it has cared for more than seventy five thousand hospital at home patients. The lesson: delivering acute care in homes works clinically, but the economics are still fragile, and providers are trimming to what pays.

On research, a few more items on loneliness. A Rush University study of more than three thousand older adults found lonelier people lost memory and thinking skills faster over up to ten years, even after counting how often they saw friends and family. And researchers reported a home sensor system that may detect loneliness from patterns of activity at home. We'll cover loneliness properly in a coming learning segment.

On policy, beyond the Medicare rule, the federal government has suspended billions of dollars in Medicaid payments to states over fraud concerns, and a California state audit found its Medicaid agency poorly equipped to stop abuse. Fraud scrutiny keeps rising across home and community care, which raises the value of agencies with clean billing.

The Resilient angle. Three thoughts. First, regulators are moving from trusting plans to measuring them: directory accuracy, network adequacy, delivered hours. Any business that produces trustworthy measurements of whether care actually happened is on the right side of that trend, and most of those are software and services. Second, the napping study is a real, but early, opening for passive measurement of sleep and daytime activity. If Resilient ever wanted a research partnership on early cognitive decline signals, this is the kind of question to bring to a university group, not a product to sell next quarter. Third, dementia care is being organized and paid for in new ways, which brings us to GUIDE.

Today's learning segment is the GUIDE model. GUIDE stands for Guiding an Improved Dementia Experience. It's an eight year Medicare pilot that started in July 2024, run by the CMS Innovation Center, and it now lists two hundred ninety two participants. Participants are Medicare enrolled providers, like health systems, physician groups, and community organizations, who build a dementia care program. It covers people with dementia in traditional Medicare, not Medicare Advantage, with special attention to people on both Medicare and Medicaid. What the program must provide: an interdisciplinary team including a dementia trained clinician and a care navigator, a care plan, a twenty four seven support line, caregiver training and support, help with health related social needs, and coordination with community services. How it pays: a monthly per patient payment that replaces some regular billing, set by tier. The tiers depend on how complex the patient is and whether there's an unpaid caregiver. For 2026, the base rates for established patients range from about sixty eight dollars a month for a low complexity patient with a caregiver to about two hundred thirty one dollars for a high complexity one, with higher rates, up to about four hundred nine dollars, in the first six months. Payments are adjusted for geography, for lower income patients, and later for quality performance, which can raise the base rate by as much as seventy five percent. And here's the piece families notice: GUIDE pays for respite, so the caregiver gets a break, up to about two thousand five hundred to two thousand six hundred dollars a year per patient for eligible patients in the moderate and high complexity tiers, through in home aides, adult day centers or short facility stays. GUIDE matters for three reasons. It's the first time traditional Medicare pays directly for caregiver support and respite. It lets organizations contract with partner organizations to deliver parts of the program, which opens the door to specialized vendors. And if the results are good, it could become a permanent Medicare benefit. The risks: the payments are modest, Medicare Advantage members aren't included, and Innovation Center models can be changed or ended early.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: a turnkey caregiver support and respite partner for GUIDE programs. Many GUIDE participants are health systems and practices that are good at medical care but struggle with the parts that happen at home: answering the twenty four seven line, training family caregivers, and arranging respite through reliable aides and adult day centers. The idea is to be their contracted partner organization in Pennsylvania and neighboring states: a dementia trained support line that answers at three in the morning, a structured caregiver training program, and a vetted respite network that bills through the GUIDE respite codes. Who pays: the GUIDE participant, out of its monthly payments, plus the respite billing itself. Why now: the model is in its third year, the new program track only started delivering services in July 2025, and many of those programs are still building their home side. Risks: the monthly payments are thin, so the price must be low; the model could be changed or ended; and only traditional Medicare patients are covered. Hardware isn't needed. Resilient's fit is its clinical operations experience and its Pittsburgh base. The first test is to pull the CMS list of GUIDE participants in Pennsylvania and ask three of them which of the required services they find hardest to deliver, and what they pay for them today.

A question to think about: when a family caregiver calls for help at three in the morning, who should answer, and who should pay for it? That's your briefing. Have a good Tuesday.
