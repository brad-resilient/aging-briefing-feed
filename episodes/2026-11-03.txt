Third, AI liability. Healthcare Dive reports the American Medical Association released a framework saying responsibility for harm from clinical AI should be shared among three groups. Doctors must use judgment and can override AI, but shouldn't be blamed for errors they can't see. AI developers are responsible for designing safe tools and telling users how they perform, and the AMA says disclaimers alone aren't enough. And the hospitals and practices that deploy AI must test it first, train staff, and monitor it for problems. Patients harmed by AI should have a path to compensation. An AMA survey found about a quarter of doctors had no AI training. Why this matters: this is guidance, not law, but it signals where malpractice and product liability are heading. Any company selling AI into elder care, including alerts or triage from a monitoring device, should expect to carry more of the liability and to document how its product performs.

The Resilient angle. Two things from today. First, the BrightSpring news is a reminder that the big buyers in home based care are moving toward taking financial risk for patients' total costs. A risk bearing home care company cares about catching problems early, which is where monitoring can genuinely help, but only if it fits their workflow and is proven. Second, scams. Fraudsters regularly pose as Medicare or medical equipment companies to steal Medicare numbers and bill for devices nobody needed. Any company selling a device to older adults should design its sales and support so a customer can tell it apart from a scammer, for example never asking for a Medicare number in an unsolicited call.

Today's learning segment is elder financial exploitation and scams. Start with the numbers. The Federal Trade Commission's latest annual report on older consumers, covering 2025, found people sixty and older reported losing about three billion dollars to fraud, up from two point four billion the year before and about five times the 2020 level. But most fraud is never reported, and the FTC estimates the true loss could be as high as about one hundred twenty billion dollars. The FBI's internet crime report for 2025 counted seven point seven billion dollars in losses for people over sixty, up about sixty percent. The FBI tied three hundred fifty two million dollars of older adults' losses to AI enabled scams, like cloned voices of grandchildren in distress and deepfake investment pitches.

What kinds of scams? By dollars, investment scams are the biggest, over a billion dollars in the FTC data, up forty two percent, often involving cryptocurrency. Then come people impersonating the government or a business, especially fake bank fraud departments, then romance scams. Older adults were more than three times as likely as younger people to report losing money to tech support scams, where a pop up says your computer is infected. The oldest are hit hardest per case: the median loss for people over eighty was two thousand dollars, the highest of any age group. And the losses are concentrated: losses over one hundred thousand dollars were only six percent of older adults' loss reports, but seventy percent of the dollars. Money mostly moves by bank wire, about a billion dollars, and cryptocurrency, about six hundred million.

Scams by strangers are only half the problem. Financial exploitation by people the older person knows, family members, caregivers, or someone holding power of attorney, is widely considered just as large and even less reported, which I'm stating from general knowledge. And there's a medical link: from memory, research shows the ability to manage money is one of the first abilities to slip in early Alzheimer's disease, often before diagnosis. Sudden changes in spending can be an early sign of cognitive decline.

What protections exist? From memory, the federal Senior Safe Act of 2018 protects trained bank and brokerage employees from liability when they report suspected exploitation, and brokerage rules let firms ask clients for a trusted contact person and place temporary holds on suspicious withdrawals. Many states require or allow banks to report suspected abuse to Adult Protective Services. But banks still struggle, because the customer is usually authorizing the transfer themselves, convinced by the scammer.

That creates a business. The leading example is Carefull, which monitors older adults' bank and card accounts with AI for more than seventy fraud patterns, and sends plain language alerts to a trusted family member without giving them account access. Its main channel is banks and credit unions, which offer it to customers as a benefit. In March it partnered with CSI, a technology provider to community banks, to reach more of them. Another company, EverSafe, does similar monitoring, from memory. The model that works is selling through the institution that holds the money, because older adults rarely buy fraud protection for themselves until after a loss.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: a scam recovery and protection service for families after a loss. The customer is the family of an older adult who has just been scammed, reached through banks, employers' caregiving benefits, elder law attorneys and Area Agencies on Aging. The problem: after a scam, families are panicked and don't know what to do. They need to report to the FBI and FTC, dispute transactions with the bank quickly, freeze credit, change passwords and phone numbers, watch for recovery scams, which target people who've already been scammed, and often set up a power of attorney or trusted contacts so it doesn't happen again. Sometimes they need to recognize that the scam was a warning sign of cognitive decline and get a medical evaluation. The service would be a case manager plus software that handles all of that in the first seventy two hours, then sets up ongoing account monitoring. Who pays: banks, as a customer remediation service, which helps their reputation and complaint numbers; employers through caregiving benefits; and families directly at a flat fee. Why now: reported losses are rising fast, AI voice cloning is making scams more convincing, and banks are under pressure to protect older customers. Risks: money is rarely recovered, so the service has to sell peace of mind and prevention, not refunds; competition from Carefull and identity theft insurance; and the service must never look like a recovery scam itself. No hardware is needed. The first test: partner with one Pittsburgh area credit union or elder law firm, take ten recent scam cases, and measure how many hours of work the family was spared and whether any money was recovered.

A question to think about: if a bank sees an eighty five year old customer wire fifty thousand dollars to a stranger, who should it call, and what would have to be in place, legally and technically, for that call to happen before the money leaves?
