Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Tuesday, November tenth. It's a light news day, so today is three short items and a longer learning segment that sits squarely in Resilient's territory. The three items: the remote monitoring industry is already planning how to use the two year delay CMS gave it; the freeze on new home health and hospice agencies hits its six month mark on Friday with no word from CMS; and home health dealmaking has slowed sharply, even as one private equity backed company keeps buying. Today's learning segment is respiratory depression and sleep apnea in older adults.

First, remote monitoring. Healthcare Dive's Anuja Vaidya reported yesterday on how the industry is reacting to the payment rule we covered last week. To recap, CMS finalized its plan to stop paying for remote patient monitoring that's run by outside contracted companies rather than a practice's own employed staff, but pushed the start from January 2027 to January 2029. CMS pointed to reports from the HHS inspector general about fraud and improper billing tied to third party monitoring arrangements. The trade groups are treating the delay as a two year lobbying window. Kyle Zebley, who leads the American Telemedicine Association, said his group will use the next two rulemaking cycles to press CMS to adopt targeted safeguards against fraud and abuse instead of a blanket ban. Brian Scarpelli of the Connected Health Initiative said his group will push CMS on what kinds of clinical staffing arrangements count before the 2029 deadline. And Caroline Pearson of the Peterson Center on Healthcare called the whole episode a cautionary tale about paying for volume, and warned the rule will create broad disruption in the market.

What it means for Resilient, and this is my analysis. The most important open question is the definition of employed clinical staff. If CMS ends up accepting arrangements like leased staff or shared services under a practice's direction, many vendor models survive with paperwork changes. If it holds to direct employment only, the market shifts toward selling software and devices to practices that run monitoring themselves. Either way, the next two physician fee schedule proposals, in July 2027 and July 2028, are where this gets decided, and companies that can show low fraud risk and real outcomes will have the strongest hand. It would be worth Resilient deciding which side of that line it wants to be on, and whether to join one of these trade groups' comment efforts.

Second, the freeze. The national moratorium on new Medicare enrollment of home health agencies and hospices took effect May thirteenth for six months, which puts the end around this Friday, November thirteenth. As of yesterday, I found no notice extending, modifying or ending it. Any extension has to be published in the Federal Register. Home Health Care News's commentator predicted a modified freeze, perhaps limited to the states with the most fraud, rather than a blanket extension or a clean lapse. I'll check again each day this week.

Third, deals. Home Health Care News reports that LiveWell Partners, a St. Louis based home health and hospice company backed by Encore Management Group and Camino Partners, bought Assured Home Health and Hospice in Detroit, its fourth deal in Michigan. Assured keeps its name and leaders. Chief executive Stacie Bratcher, who we heard from last week complaining that the Medicare raise doesn't keep up with wages, said each Michigan partner has strengthened LiveWell's ability to serve patients and referral partners across the region. Now the learning segment: respiratory depression and sleep apnea in older adults. Much of this is from memory and general clinical knowledge, because the key research pages wouldn't load today, so treat the specific numbers as approximate and worth checking.

Start with sleep apnea. Obstructive sleep apnea is when the airway repeatedly collapses during sleep, so breathing stops or gets shallow, oxygen drops, and the brain briefly wakes the person to restart breathing. It becomes much more common with age. Studies suggest that a large share of people over sixty five have at least mild sleep apnea, and most of them have never been diagnosed. In older adults it often doesn't look like the classic loud snoring, overweight middle aged man. It can show up as daytime sleepiness, nighttime confusion, getting up to urinate at night, high blood pressure that's hard to control, atrial fibrillation, or memory problems. Untreated sleep apnea is linked to heart disease, stroke, falls and car crashes, and there's growing evidence of a link with cognitive decline.

Now respiratory depression. That's when breathing becomes too slow or too shallow to clear carbon dioxide and keep oxygen up, usually because of drugs that suppress the brain's drive to breathe, mainly opioids, benzodiazepines, gabapentinoids and anesthesia. As we discussed yesterday, older adults are more sensitive to these drugs and often take several at once. Combine those drugs with undiagnosed sleep apnea, and the risk multiplies, because the drugs blunt the brain's ability to wake up and restart breathing during an apnea.

The best data come from hospitals after surgery. A large international study called PRODIGY, published around 2020, put continuous monitoring on patients receiving opioids on regular hospital floors and found that close to half had at least one episode of respiratory depression, most of them not noticed by staff using routine spot checks every few hours. The risk score from that study includes being sixty or older, being male, not being used to opioids, having a sleep disorder and having heart failure. Episodes cluster at night and in the first day or so after surgery. Safety groups such as the Anesthesia Patient Safety Foundation have long recommended continuous monitoring, using pulse oximetry and, when patients are on oxygen, capnography, which measures exhaled carbon dioxide. Many hospitals still don't do it on regular floors, mainly because of cost, false alarms and staffing.

The gap is even bigger after discharge. Older patients go home after surgery with opioid prescriptions, often to sleep alone, with no monitoring at all. Nursing homes and assisted living rarely monitor breathing overnight either.

Treatment for sleep apnea has also changed. CPAP machines remain the standard, and from memory Medicare requires people to show they use CPAP at least four hours a night on most nights early on to keep coverage, which many older adults struggle with. There are implanted nerve stimulators for people who can't tolerate CPAP. And the weight loss drug tirzepatide, sold as Zepbound, was approved for sleep apnea in people with obesity in late 2024, which brings drug companies into the diagnosis business.

What it means for Resilient, and this is my analysis. There are three settings where unrecognized breathing problems in older adults meet a real payer or provider budget: the hospital floor after surgery, the first nights at home after discharge, and long term care. In each, the hardest part isn't the sensor. It's who watches the alerts, what they do at three in the morning, and who pays for that response. That's also exactly the question the new remote monitoring rule forces.

Today's opportunity, and I'll be upfront that it uses a device, because finding sleep apnea genuinely requires measuring breathing during sleep. A sleep apnea find and treat program for older adults in senior living and Medicare Advantage. A coordinator screens residents or members with a short questionnaire, like the widely used STOP BANG screen, and reviews medication lists for sedatives. People who screen positive do a home sleep test, which Medicare covers, read by a sleep physician partner. Those diagnosed get treatment and, most importantly, adherence coaching for the first ninety days, since that's when most people give up on CPAP. The sedative review from yesterday's idea fits naturally alongside. Who pays: Medicare covers the testing and the equipment through normal billing; senior living operators and Medicare Advantage plans would pay for the coordination and coaching because untreated apnea drives falls, nighttime confusion, heart problems and move outs to higher levels of care. Why now: overdose and sedative risks in older adults are rising, drug makers are pushing sleep apnea diagnosis, and accountable care organizations have new incentives to prevent hospital stays. Risks: adherence is hard, sleep physician capacity is limited, and the outcome benefits in very old adults are less proven than in middle age. A wearable that tracks breathing and oxygen overnight could be used for screening or for follow up, but the business works with standard home sleep tests too. The first test: one Pittsburgh senior living community, screen everyone willing, test those at risk, and track how many are diagnosed, how many stick with treatment at ninety days, and falls and nighttime incidents over six months.

A question to think about: when a sensor detects a dangerous drop in breathing at three in the morning in someone's home, who should get the alert, a family member, a nurse call center, or 911, and who should pay for that person to be awake? That's your briefing. Have a good Tuesday.
