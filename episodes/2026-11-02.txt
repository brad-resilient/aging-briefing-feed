Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Monday, November second. Three things today. A federal judge threw out Medicare's narrow definition of who counts as too sick to meet the new Medicaid work requirements. Medicare's big end of year payment rules still haven't come out, and the clock is running on the home health freeze and the California crackdown. And Zocdoc has opened its booking system to AI agents, a small step toward software that handles the logistics of care, which is today's learning segment: care coordination software for families.

First, Medicaid work requirements. Healthcare Dive reports that on Thursday, Judge Richard Stearns in Massachusetts vacated the Medicare and Medicaid agency's definition of medically frail, the category of people exempt from the new work rule. He found the agency had defined it too narrowly, requiring people to be so sick they couldn't comply rather than simply very sick, and he granted the twenty five states and Washington D C a permanent injunction. He left in place two other limits: states can only look back twelve months in medical records, and there are limits on how often people can attest to their own frailty. Starting in 2027, adults covered through the Medicaid expansion must log eighty hours a month of work, volunteering or school. The agency declined to comment and could appeal, and a second lawsuit from patients and provider groups is still pending. Why this matters: many people in their fifties and early sixties on Medicaid have chronic illness, and many are caring for older relatives. The broader the exemptions, the fewer people lose coverage, and the less pressure on families and safety net providers. One gap worth noting: the article doesn't say whether family caregivers get an exemption, so I'll watch for that.

Second, the rules and deadlines. Last year the final physician payment rule came out on October thirty first. This year, as of this morning, it still hasn't published. That's the rule that decides whether Medicare stops paying for remote patient monitoring run by outside vendors, requires a face to face visit first, and consolidates the billing codes. The final home health payment rule is also still pending. Meanwhile, the notice expanding Medicare's home health claims review program to California is due to publish today, with a fourteen day comment window. And the national freeze on new home health and hospice agencies expires in eleven days unless Medicare extends it. Tomorrow is Election Day, which affects state Medicaid budgets and Congress. Why this matters: the next two weeks will set the rules for remote monitoring and home health in 2027. If you have any plans that depend on remote monitoring billing, I'd hold them until the final rule is out.

Third, AI agents booking care. Healthcare Dive reports that Zocdoc, the doctor booking site, now lets AI agents search for doctors, check insurance and book appointments for patients, with the patient's consent. Google Gemini, Amazon's health AI and a calling and texting agent called Instinct are already connected, and Zocdoc says AI agent traffic on its site has grown almost thirty fold since July. Agents must get explicit consent before booking or canceling, and can't train on or resell the data, though Zocdoc didn't say how it will enforce that. Why this matters: one of the hardest parts of caring for an aging parent is the endless scheduling and phone calls. When booking systems open up to software agents, an AI that does that work on a family's behalf becomes possible. More on that in the learning segment.

On the social side, I couldn't reach Reddit or Hacker News directly, so nothing reliable to report.

The Resilient angle. Any device that monitors an older adult produces alerts and data, and the question is always where they land. Today they usually go to a monitoring center or a doctor's portal. But the person who actually acts is often an adult child juggling a job. If the family uses a coordination tool, a device alert should show up there, with a clear next step. So care coordination platforms and caregiver benefit companies are a potential distribution channel and partner for any monitoring product, Resilient's included.

Today's learning segment is care coordination software for families. Start with the job. A family caring for an aging parent has to schedule appointments, track medications, deal with insurance and pharmacy problems, arrange rides and home care, share updates among siblings, manage bills and legal paperwork, and find services. Studies of caregivers, from memory, find they spend many hours a month on this logistics work, separate from hands on care.

There are roughly four generations of products. The first is free shared calendars and update boards, like CaringBridge, a nonprofit for sharing health updates, Lotsa Helping Hands and Caring Village, which help a circle of family and friends sign up for tasks. They're useful, but families rarely pay for them, so many similar apps have shut down or stayed small. That's from memory and general industry knowledge.

The second generation is human care concierges sold through employers. The leader is Wellthy, which TechCrunch reported has raised about seventy seven million dollars, works with about three hundred fifty employers, including six of the ten largest companies, and grew to about two million covered lives by 2022. Families get a dedicated care coordinator, often a social worker, who books appointments, arranges transportation, finds equipment and handles paperwork. This spring, Wellthy partnered with Maven Clinic to offer a combined family care benefit aimed at the sandwich generation. Competitors include Cariloop, ianacare and Homethrive. The model is that employers pay a fee per employee per month, because caregiving drives absenteeism and turnover. The challenge is that only a small share of eligible employees use it, and employers keep asking for proof that it saves money.

The third generation is clinical programs that pay for coordination. The most important is Medicare's GUIDE model for dementia, which started in July 2024. From memory, it pays participating practices a monthly amount per patient to provide a care navigator, a support line that's open around the clock, caregiver training, and up to about twenty five hundred dollars a year of respite care. It's the first time traditional Medicare pays directly for supporting the family caregiver. Some Medicare Advantage and Medicaid plans also run caregiver programs. Software vendors increasingly sell to these programs rather than to families.

The fourth generation is just starting: AI agents that actually do the tasks. The biggest barrier has been that the data and the actions are trapped. One caregiver app, Helpful, told a reporter it had to scrape insurance websites to show families their benefits, because there's no data connection. That's changing. Zocdoc now accepts AI agents for booking, Medicare and big insurers are being pushed to open data through interfaces, and voice AI can make phone calls. So the lesson of twenty years of caregiver apps is that organizing the work isn't enough. The value is in doing the work, which used to require a human coordinator and now may be partly done by AI.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: an AI caregiving agent that does the logistics, with a human coordinator behind it. The customer is the employer, as a caregiving benefit, or a Medicare Advantage or GUIDE program that wants to support family caregivers more cheaply. The problem: human concierge services like Wellthy work but are expensive and lightly used, and families drown in calls, forms and scheduling. The product: a caregiver tells the agent, by text or voice, what they need, such as book Mom's cardiology follow up, refill her prescriptions, find out why the claim was denied, or schedule a home care aide for Tuesday. The agent does it, through booking systems like Zocdoc, phone calls by voice AI, and online portals, checks in for approval at key steps, and hands anything sensitive or stuck to a human coordinator. Who pays: employers at a lower per employee price than human concierge services, or health plans and dementia care programs per family served. Why now: booking and insurance systems are opening to AI agents, voice AI can handle routine calls, and employers want caregiving benefits that people actually use. Risks: errors with medical consequences, so the agent should never give clinical or dosing advice; consent and privacy, especially acting on behalf of a parent who may have dementia, which requires proper legal authority like a health care proxy; and established competitors adding AI themselves. No hardware is needed. The first test: offer it free to twenty Pittsburgh families caring for a parent for sixty days, log every task, and measure how many the agent completed without a human, and how many hours it saved each family.

A question to think about: if an AI agent can book appointments and handle insurance calls for a family, what is the remaining job of a human care coordinator, and who would pay for that? That's your briefing. Have a good week.
