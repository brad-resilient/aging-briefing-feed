Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Friday, October sixteenth. Today is all about people, because the binding constraint in elder care isn't technology, it's hands. Three things. A startup that turns family members into paid, certified caregivers just raised eighty two million dollars. New numbers show how short the care workforce really is, from Maine to the nursing home bed count. And the waiting list for Medicaid home care hit about six hundred fifty thousand people. Today's learning segment is the direct care workforce, and today's opportunity of the day has no device in it at all.

First, Abby Care. On Thursday it announced an eighty two million dollar Series C led by Transformation Capital, with CVS Health Ventures joining existing investors Khosla, Thrive and Sequoia. Abby trains family members to become certified caregivers, employs them, and bills Medicaid and health plans for the care they provide. It started with children who need home therapy and private duty nursing. The CVS investor gave a number worth remembering: traditional private duty nursing programs fill only about sixty five percent of the shifts that are authorized and paid for. In other words, the money is already approved, but nobody shows up. Abby's bet is that the person most likely to show up is already in the house. Why this matters to you: this is a business built on the workforce problem, not on hardware, and serious investors are paying for it. The same model works for older adults in states that let Medicaid pay family members, and that's where today's opportunity comes from.

Second, the workforce numbers. In Maine, about twenty three thousand five hundred hours of Medicaid home care go unfilled every week, and the state needs more than two thousand additional direct care workers. Nonprofit agencies there can afford to pay about one hundred twenty five percent of the minimum wage, and lawmakers want to raise that to one hundred forty percent, which would be about twenty two dollars an hour. Nursing homes are shrinking too. KFF Health News reports that national nursing home capacity fell about five percent from 2019 to 2024, there are over nine hundred fewer certified facilities than in 2015, and in 2024 almost half of nursing homes were limiting admissions. The median nursing home aide earns about twenty one dollars an hour, and about forty percent of aides rely on public assistance themselves. And immigration matters. An advocacy group's report this summer estimated that immigrants are about forty percent of the home health workforce nationally, around sixty percent in Florida and seventy four percent in New York, so stricter enforcement lands directly on care capacity. Why this matters: every part of the system is limited by staff, so any business that creates caregiver capacity, or makes each caregiver go further, has a market.

Third, KFF's annual survey of Medicaid home care waiting lists. About six hundred fifty thousand people are on waiting lists in forty states, up seven percent from last year, and the average wait is now thirty four months. About a quarter are older adults or people with physical disabilities. Most of the rest have intellectual or developmental disabilities. One detail matters for business: more than eighty percent of people on these lists already qualify for basic personal care services while they wait. Many families just don't know how to get them.

On research, a JAMA study from USC published last month found that the share of older Americans taking five or more prescription drugs rose from thirty one to almost thirty six percent between 2016 and 2023, and more than one in five are taking combinations with a risk of a major drug interaction, most often involving antidepressants, statins and blood thinners. Medication management is a large, unsexy problem that pharmacists and software can attack.

On the social side, the conversation is about staffing. A STAT opinion piece argued that the nursing shortage can't be fixed just by training more nurses, because people leave over working conditions. The writer's image was pouring water into a bucket with a hole in it. Home care workers and direct support workers are the most overlooked part of that bucket.

The Resilient angle. The lesson this week is that people, not devices, are the scarce resource in elder care. The businesses that win are the ones that create caregiver capacity, like Abby, or that help families get benefits they already qualify for. Resilient's useful assets here are less about hardware and more about running clinical programs, navigating regulation, and knowing the Pennsylvania market. Technology belongs where it helps one caregiver do more, such as scheduling, documentation, training, or remote check ins, and nowhere else.

Today's learning segment is the direct care workforce. These are the home care aides, personal care aides, home health aides and nursing assistants who do the hands on work of bathing, dressing, feeding and moving people. There are roughly five million of them in the United States, which makes it one of the largest occupations in the country. That's an approximate figure from the workforce research group PHI. They are mostly women, disproportionately women of color, and a large share are immigrants. Pay is low, typically around seventeen to twenty one dollars an hour, and many work part time without benefits. Turnover in home care agencies is very high, often estimated at well over half the workforce every year. That's an approximate figure and worth checking. Demand keeps rising: the Bureau of Labor Statistics projects home health and personal care aide jobs will grow about seventeen percent over the next decade, more than five times the average for all jobs. Why are wages so low? For most of this work, Medicaid is the payer, and the reimbursement rate the state sets puts a ceiling on what agencies can pay. When retail and warehouse jobs pay more, workers leave. That's why the real policy fights are about Medicaid rates and wage floors, and why a new federal rule requiring that most Medicaid home care payments go to worker pay has been so contested. For you, the takeaways are simple. Labor is the constraint. Medicaid sets the price. And there are three ways to win: bring in new workers, often family members; keep the workers you have; or let each worker cover more people safely.

Finally, the opportunity of the day, with no device in it: a paid family caregiver agency for older adults. Many states let Medicaid pay a relative, often an adult child, to care for an older parent, through programs with names like structured family caregiving or participant directed services. The rules vary, and many families never hear about them. The business would find eligible families, including people sitting on home care waiting lists who already qualify for basic personal care, help them enroll, train and certify the family caregiver, handle the required visit verification and documentation with good software, and bill Medicaid or the health plan as the agency. Revenue is the agency share of each hour billed. Why now: there aren't enough aides, Abby just proved investors will fund this for children, and the waiting lists keep growing. The risks are real. State rules differ, some states don't let spouses be paid, Medicaid cuts could lower rates, and fraud scrutiny of home care is intense. Pennsylvania is a natural place to start, since you know the market. The first test is to map which Pennsylvania programs pay family caregivers of older adults today, at what hourly rate, and how many families use them.

A question to think about today: if labor is the real constraint in elder care, what is the most valuable thing Resilient could build that has nothing to do with a sensor? That's your briefing. Have a good weekend.
