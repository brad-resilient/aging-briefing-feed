It's earnings season for senior care companies, so today leans on what their quarterly calls reveal about where the money is going. Three things. Real estate investment trusts, the big landlords of elder care, are piling into senior housing and selling off medical buildings and nursing homes to do it. Brookdale, the largest senior living operator, says occupancy is rising more slowly than it hoped and is cutting back on paid referral services to find new residents. And home health and nursing home companies posted big growth numbers this week, mostly by buying other companies, even as they warn about the new fraud rules. Then a look at the week ahead, and today's learning segment on senior housing landlords and private equity, which ties directly to the first story.

First, the landlords. Senior Housing News reports that National Healthcare Properties, a publicly traded real estate trust, is selling eighty six outpatient medical buildings for about five hundred twenty eight million dollars and putting the proceeds into senior housing. It has bought twenty five senior housing communities this year, for sixty in total. Its chief executive, Michael Anderson, said on Thursday's earnings call that all of the company's operating income will come from senior housing properties. Same store income at those properties rose almost fourteen percent from a year ago. American Healthcare REIT tells a similar story. Its senior housing operating income grew twenty eight percent from a year earlier at the same properties, it raised its full year outlook, and it spent about one point seven billion dollars on seventeen communities last quarter. Its president, Gabe Willhite, said the goal is not to stay at ninety percent occupancy, but to go higher. Earlier this year, Welltower, the biggest of these landlords, said it planned to sell about one point three billion dollars of nursing home properties to focus on senior housing, according to a February deal summary. And LTC Properties and Omega, which we mentioned earlier in the week, are also growing their senior housing holdings.

Why the rush? Demand is rising as the baby boomers reach their eighties, very little new senior housing was built during the pandemic and the years of high interest rates, so occupancy and rents are climbing. Nursing homes, by contrast, depend on Medicaid and Medicare rates the landlords can't control. My read: the money is going to private pay senior housing, where rents can rise, and away from anything that depends on government payment. That shapes which customers will have budgets for new services over the next few years.

Second, Brookdale. Brookdale Senior Living, which runs several hundred communities across the country, reported earnings this week. Senior Housing News reports occupancy was eighty three point one percent last quarter, up a bit, but the company now expects the full year to come in below eighty three percent, and it cut its profit outlook. The stock fell almost seventeen percent that day. The most interesting part is the strategy shift. Brookdale is moving away from leads bought from third party referral services and building its own sales channels. Chief executive Nick Stengle called it a paradigm shift, and said it's an enduring way forward, not a one off. Referral services, the best known is A Place for Mom, which I'm naming from memory, typically charge communities a large fee for each resident who moves in. Brookdale is also buying thirty communities it currently leases from Welltower for two hundred ninety million dollars. The number of Brookdale communities above ninety percent occupancy rose to one hundred eighty six, and the number below seventy percent fell to sixty nine. So the strong buildings are getting stronger while the weak ones are still struggling.

Third, the home care and nursing home earnings. The Pennant Group, which runs home health and hospice agencies, reported revenue of three hundred two million dollars, up thirty two percent, largely from fifty four locations it bought when UnitedHealth had to sell some of Amedisys's agencies as a condition of buying Amedisys. Home health admissions were up thirty nine percent. Chief executive Brent Guerisoli welcomed the two point four percent Medicare raise as a step in the right direction, but said reimbursement must account for high inflation, and warned that the new fraud rules, like revoking a company's Medicare enrollment back to the date problems began, add real burdens even for honest providers. On the nursing home side, Skilled Nursing News reports that PACS Group took over thirty one nursing homes from the Eduro deal, then bought thirty two more in Florida and eleven facilities in Texas. Revenue was one point five billion dollars, up eleven and a half percent, with occupancy above ninety one percent. The article notes PACS still faces ongoing government investigations. The pattern across all of these: growth is coming from consolidation. Bigger operators are buying smaller ones, and the new home health rule, which makes new owners re-enroll and pass a survey, will make that slower and costlier.

Now the week ahead. The national freeze on new home health and hospice agencies enrolling in Medicare is due to expire around November thirteenth, based on our earlier reporting. Home Health Care News laid out the options: extend it, let it lapse, limit it to certain regions, or exempt established providers with clean records, a so called gold card, which CMS hasn't announced. I found no new reporting on the decision this week, so watch for an announcement. Medicare open enrollment runs through December seventh, and with more than four million people losing their current plans, expect stories about confused seniors and plan switching. And Congress returns for its lame duck session, where the scheduled cut to Medicare doctor pay is a likely bipartisan target. Now the learning segment: senior housing landlords and private equity in elder care. Here's how the money works. Most senior housing and nursing home buildings are owned by one company and run by another. The owner is often a real estate investment trust, a publicly traded company that must pay out most of its income to shareholders, like Welltower, Ventas, Omega or American Healthcare REIT. The operator, like Brookdale, Sunrise or a regional company, hires staff and runs the community.

There are two main deal structures. Under a lease, the operator pays the landlord fixed rent, and keeps any profit or eats any loss. That's what Brookdale has been doing with Welltower. Under what's called a RIDEA structure, named after a 2007 law, the landlord owns the operations too and pays the operator a management fee, so the landlord gets the upside when occupancy and rents rise. In the industry this is called a senior housing operating portfolio, or SHOP, and you heard that term throughout today's stories. The landlords love SHOP right now because occupancy is rising, so they capture the growth directly.

Where does private equity fit? Private equity firms buy companies with a lot of borrowed money, aim to improve profits, and sell within about five to seven years. In elder care, they've bought home health and hospice agencies, nursing home chains, and senior living operators. Research on nursing homes, from memory, has linked private equity ownership to lower staffing and worse outcomes on average, and to arrangements where the operator sells its buildings and then leases them back, so the money is taken out up front and the rent burden stays with the operator. That's why Senior Housing News quoted an industry consultant this week saying a Democratic Congress means more scrutiny of capital providers, and why CMS now requires more ownership disclosure from nursing homes and home health agencies. One more thing worth noticing: this week, one senior housing chief executive said heavily borrowed buyers are taking a seat on the sidelines, which suggests real estate trusts with cheaper money are outbidding private equity right now.

What this means for selling into senior living. The decision maker for buying a new service is often the operator, but in a SHOP arrangement the landlord feels the financial result directly, and landlords like Welltower increasingly push data and technology programs across their operators. So for anything that raises occupancy, keeps residents longer, or lowers staffing costs, the landlord can be a second buyer and a distribution channel to many operators at once.

Today's opportunity, and it doesn't need hardware. A hospital to senior living transition service that replaces paid referral fees. Hospitals have older patients who are medically ready to leave but stuck for days because they can't go home safely and nobody has arranged assisted living or a care plan. Senior living operators like Brookdale want move ins without paying large referral fees. The service would put trained transition coordinators, with a nurse on call, inside hospitals and physician groups. They'd help families choose a community based on care needs and budget, handle the paperwork and medication handoff, and check in for the first thirty days so the move sticks. Who pays: hospitals, for fewer stuck bed days, and operators, a flat per move in fee lower than what referral services charge, or a subscription from a landlord across its portfolio. Why now: Brookdale is publicly walking away from referral leads, landlords are pouring money into senior housing and need occupancy, and hospitals are under pressure on length of stay. Risks: conflicts of interest if operators pay, so the service must disclose fees and show families more than one option; hospital discharge teams may see it as a competitor; and many patients can't afford private pay assisted living, so a Medicaid waiver track is needed. The first test: partner with one Pittsburgh hospital and two or three senior living operators, handle thirty discharges over three months, and measure days saved in the hospital, move ins, and how many residents are still there after ninety days.

A question to think about: if landlords now capture the upside of senior housing operations directly, should a company selling into senior living pitch the operator who runs the building, or the landlord who owns the profit? That's your briefing. Have a good Saturday.
