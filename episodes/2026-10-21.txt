Medicare Advantage is in its third straight year of turmoil, with more than three million people losing their plans for 2027. Medicare's rules for remote patient monitoring are about to change, possibly a lot, with a final decision due in the next few weeks. And a forty seven year study shows physical capacity starts declining in our mid thirties, but people who get active later still gain. Today's learning segment fits the second story: the billing codes behind remote monitoring and care management, and who actually makes money from them.

First, Medicare Advantage. According to CMS data analyzed by Medicare Market Insights, nearly three point two million Medicare Advantage members, about eleven percent, must pick new plans for 2027 because their insurer is dropping their plan. That's up from about two point nine million last year and two million the year before. Before 2024, only one or two percent lost their plan in a typical year. Fortune reports Humana is ending plans for about six hundred thousand people, roughly eight percent of its members, and UnitedHealthcare about three hundred ninety thousand. KFF finds that six hundred thirty eight thousand people in one hundred ninety three counties will have no Medicare Advantage plan with drug coverage at all. For people who stay, costs are rising: the average maximum out of pocket limit is up nine percent to about six thousand six hundred dollars, and the average drug deductible is up twenty three percent to about five hundred nine dollars. The one category growing fast is chronic condition special needs plans, which have roughly doubled since 2025. And not every plan is retreating. Alignment Health, a fast growing Medicare Advantage plan, just signed Astrana Health's network of about three thousand primary care doctors and sixty five hundred specialists across seven California counties, starting January, with a focus on doctors who speak Chinese, Korean and Vietnamese. Why this matters: the market is splitting. Big national plans are shedding unprofitable members, while focused plans built around specific conditions or communities are growing. Those focused plans are often the best customers for services that manage chronic illness at home.

Second, remote patient monitoring. In July, CMS proposed the biggest changes to remote monitoring payment since the codes were created, and the final rule usually comes out in early November, to take effect January first. Three proposals stand out. First, Medicare would only pay when the clinical staff doing the monitoring are employed by the billing practice, not supplied by an outside company. The American Telemedicine Association says sixty to seventy percent of practices now use contracted staff. Second, a face to face visit to start monitoring would be required. Third, the device supply payment would drop sharply, from roughly fifty two dollars a month to roughly eighteen. One remote monitoring company, ChartSpan, estimates total payment per patient per month would fall about sixty five percent, though that's its own estimate and it has an interest in the outcome. CMS is also floating a future plan to collapse seventeen monitoring codes into four bundles. Why now: federal inspectors have repeatedly found that many patients billed for monitoring weren't getting all the parts of the service, and the vendor model, where companies supply the device and the nurses and split the payment with the practice, grew very fast. Why this matters for you: if Resilient has ever modeled revenue around Medicare remote monitoring codes, those assumptions may be out of date in a few weeks. If the rule is finalized as proposed, the winners are practices and health systems that run monitoring with their own staff, and companies that help them do that.

Third, research on how we age. A Swedish study from the Karolinska Institute followed four hundred twenty seven people born in 1958, testing their fitness and strength from age sixteen to sixty three. Aerobic fitness and endurance peaked between about twenty six and thirty six, explosive power even earlier, and by sixty three average performance was thirty to forty eight percent below peak, with men and women declining at similar rates. The encouraging part: people who became active as adults still improved their capacity by about five to ten percent. And from Columbia University, a study in Nature Aging found that some people who carry the highest risk Alzheimer's gene, APOE four, but stay sharp have a rare variant in a gene called fibronectin that protects the blood brain barrier. In mice, reducing fibronectin improved the barrier. It's early, but it points to a new kind of drug target.

On policy, the Medicare Advantage directory rule we covered yesterday and the remote monitoring rule both point the same way: Medicare is paying for care that can be proven to happen, delivered by accountable staff.

The Resilient angle. This is a day where the hardware question is front and center, so let's be precise. Remote monitoring is the reimbursement path most device companies point to, and it's being tightened and cut. That doesn't make devices worthless, but it means a device business shouldn't depend on Medicare monitoring codes, and the value is shifting from the device to the clinical staff and workflow around it. The other half of today's news, Medicare Advantage turmoil, favors condition focused plans and value based groups that are paid to keep people well. Those buyers care about outcomes and total cost, not billing codes, and they'll buy services or software as readily as devices.

Today's learning segment is remote monitoring and care management billing. There are four families of Medicare codes. Remote physiologic monitoring, usually called RPM, pays for a device that automatically sends readings like blood pressure, weight or oxygen levels, with separate codes for setup, for supplying the device each month, and for clinical staff time spent reviewing data and talking with the patient. In 2026 Medicare added codes for shorter periods, for two to fifteen days of readings and ten minutes of staff time, which made the codes easier to bill. Remote therapeutic monitoring, or RTM, is similar but covers things like therapy adherence, breathing and musculoskeletal status, and can be billed by physical therapists. Chronic care management pays for at least twenty minutes a month of care coordination for patients with two or more chronic conditions, no device required. And since 2025 there's Advanced Primary Care Management, a simpler monthly payment for practices that take on ongoing responsibility for a patient, tiered by how complex the patient is, with no time tracking. Here's who profits. Under the current rules, a typical arrangement is that a vendor ships a cellular device, supplies nurses or health coaches to watch the data, and the practice bills Medicare and shares the revenue. That model let RPM grow very quickly, from a niche to roughly a million Medicare patients, but federal inspectors flagged that many patients didn't get all components, like education or treatment management, and that billing was concentrated among a small number of companies. The proposed changes go right at that model. The lesson for anyone in this market: billing codes are a fragile foundation. Durable businesses either take risk for outcomes, through Medicare Advantage, special needs plans or programs like GUIDE and PACE, or they sell to organizations that do. Some figures here, including the number of Medicare monitoring patients, come from industry estimates, so treat them as approximate.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: an in house care management conversion service for primary care practices. If the employed staff rule is finalized in early November, practices that rely on outside monitoring staff will have about seven weeks to bring that work in house or stop. Many will want to keep the care management revenue but shift toward codes that don't depend on devices, like chronic care management and Advanced Primary Care Management. The idea is a service that helps a practice hire and train its own care management nurses or medical assistants, set up the workflows and documentation, choose which patients belong in which program, and stay compliant. It's paid as a fixed setup fee plus a monthly support fee, not a share of Medicare revenue, which also keeps it clear of the arrangements regulators dislike. Who pays: independent primary care groups and small health systems, starting in western Pennsylvania. Why now: a hard deadline, and a payment cut that forces practices to rethink. Risks: CMS might soften the rule, existing monitoring vendors will pivot to the same service, and small practices have little cash. Hardware isn't needed at all, though the practice may keep using devices for some patients. Resilient's fit is its regulatory and clinical quality experience, plus the fact that this is the market its own devices would sell into, so the learning is valuable either way. The first test is to call three independent practices in the Pittsburgh area that run remote monitoring through a vendor and ask what they'll do if the rule is finalized.

A question to think about: if Medicare stops paying much for the device, what part of remote monitoring is still worth paying for, and who should provide it? That's your briefing. Have a good Wednesday.
