First, the next three weeks. The midterm elections are on November third, and KFF Health News reports Medicaid cuts from last year's federal tax law have become a campaign issue, with most of the cuts timed to take effect after the vote. The results will shape how hard states push on home and community care spending next year. Then, usually around the start of November, CMS publishes the final physician fee schedule for 2027. As we covered last week, the proposed version would tighten remote patient monitoring rules and cut the payment for the device portion of remote monitoring sharply. If you've built any revenue model on those codes, this is the week to have it ready to update. And finally, the national freeze on new Medicare home health and hospice agencies, which started May thirteenth, runs out on November thirteenth unless CMS extends it in six month steps. Earlier this month CMS hadn't said what it would do, and it has said it's under no obligation to give advance notice. Trade groups, including the National Alliance for Care at Home and LeadingAge, which first supported the freeze, now want it to lapse, saying fraud is concentrated in a few areas. The Alliance says thirty eight percent of Medicare patients referred to home health after a hospital stay never got it in the third quarter of last year. Why this matters: all three decisions land within about ten days of each other, and together they set the rules for home based care going into 2027.

Second, hospital at home. Congress extended Medicare's hospital at home waiver through 2030 in the appropriations law passed this year. According to the American Medical Association, three hundred sixty six programs at one hundred thirty nine health systems in thirty seven states have been approved. The evidence is getting better. A study in JAMA Network Open this spring looked at about sixteen thousand traditional Medicare patients at hospitals with the waiver. About forty two hundred were treated at home and about eleven thousand seven hundred in the hospital. Deaths during the stay were point four percent at home versus three point six percent in the hospital, ICU transfers were three and a half percent versus about eight percent, and emergency visits were a bit lower, with modestly lower thirty day spending. But readmissions were about the same, around eleven to twelve percent in both groups. A big caution: patients are chosen for hospital at home because they're stable enough, so some of that difference is selection, not the model itself. A separate study in the Journal of the American Geriatrics Society looked at about nine hundred hospital at home stays for Medicaid patients at four Massachusetts hospitals. Eight percent were readmitted within thirty days, nine percent had to be moved back to the hospital, and the median total cost per stay was about eleven thousand six hundred dollars. Why this matters: hospital at home is now a stable, growing channel with five years of certainty, and it runs on exactly the things you'd expect: remote monitoring, nurse and paramedic visits, logistics, and family support.

Third, skilled nursing at home. Many older patients leave the hospital needing a few weeks of rehab or nursing, which usually means a skilled nursing facility. Nursing home staffing shortages have made that bottleneck worse. A 2022 American Hospital Association analysis found hospital stays for patients waiting for skilled nursing grew about twenty four percent over three years. The idea of skilled nursing at home is to deliver that rehab in the patient's own home, with therapists, aides, nurse visits and remote monitoring. The problem is that traditional Medicare has no way to pay for it, so programs mostly run where a health system owns an insurance plan or has a bundled payment deal. UMass Chan Medical School is running a randomized trial with a six and a half million dollar state grant, aiming for six hundred fifty patients. And Contessa Health, a Nashville company, runs a rehabilitation at home program with five health systems, including Allegheny Health Network right in Pittsburgh. The evidence so far is mostly company data and anecdotes, and Contessa's model depends heavily on unpaid family caregivers.

The Resilient angle. Hospital at home is one of the few places in elder care where continuous vital sign monitoring is genuinely part of the job, not something forced in. Patients are acutely ill, at home, and someone has to know when they're getting worse. So this is a legitimate place for Resilient's hardware, though hospitals already buy monitoring kits from established vendors, so the device alone won't win. The bigger and less crowded need is everything around the device: getting supplies, aides and family ready in the home, and covering the hours between visits. That's where today's opportunity sits.

Today's learning segment is hospital at home and skilled nursing at home. Hospital at home means a patient who would otherwise be admitted to the hospital, for something like pneumonia, heart failure, a COPD flare or a skin infection, is treated at home instead. They usually get at least two in person visits a day from nurses or paramedics, a daily physician or advanced practice clinician visit, often by video, remote monitoring of vital signs, and the ability to get someone to the home within about thirty minutes if something goes wrong. Those requirements come from the Medicare waiver, and I'm quoting them from memory, so check the details. The hospital is paid the same as an inpatient stay, which is what makes the economics work. The concept goes back to Johns Hopkins research in the 1990s and was spread by Mount Sinai and Brigham and Women's before the pandemic waiver in 2020 made it scalable. Who does well: patients who are stable enough, have a safe home, often a caregiver, and live within the program's service area. Who doesn't: people needing intensive care, frequent imaging or surgery, or people living alone with dementia. The business challenges are volume, because many programs only have a handful of patients at a time, staffing and travel time, logistics like delivering oxygen, IV drugs and meals, and getting doctors to refer. Skilled nursing at home is the next step down: post hospital rehab and nursing at home for a few weeks, instead of a nursing facility. The case for it is strong: patients move more and recover in their real environment, and roughly one in four nursing facility patients goes back to the hospital within thirty days. But without a Medicare payment model, it survives only in insurer owned systems, Medicare Advantage deals and pilots.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: a home readiness and in home support service for hospital at home and rehab at home programs, starting in Pittsburgh. The customer is a health system running a hospital at home or rehab at home program, such as the systems in western Pennsylvania, and Medicare Advantage plans paying for those episodes. The problem: these programs struggle with everything that isn't doctoring, including getting the home safe and ready, delivering equipment and supplies, providing aide coverage and overnight check ins between nurse visits, and training the family caregiver who often makes the model work. The service would handle that last mile per episode: a home readiness visit within hours of referral, equipment setup and pickup, trained aides on call, caregiver training, and a single coordinator for the family. Who pays: the health system, per episode, out of the inpatient level payment it already receives, or the Medicare Advantage plan for rehab at home episodes. Why now: five years of certainty for hospital at home, nursing facility bottlenecks pushing systems toward rehab at home, and growing evidence. The risks: patient volumes per program are small, so you need several health system customers; systems may build this in house or buy from established vendors like Contessa or DispatchHealth's partners; and aide staffing is the same scarce labor everyone is chasing. On hardware, an honest note: monitoring is genuinely required in hospital at home, so Resilient's device could be an optional part of the package, but the service should stand on its own and work with whatever monitoring kit the hospital already uses. The first test is to ask Allegheny Health Network and one other western Pennsylvania system what slows their hospital at home and rehab at home programs down, and what they pay today for logistics and aide coverage per episode.
