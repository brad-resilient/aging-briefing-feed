Medicare finalized next year's physician payment rule, and the part that matters most to you is remote patient monitoring: the ban on outside vendors delivering monitoring services was finalized but pushed back two years, to 2029. Medicare also finalized next year's home health rule, with a two point four percent raise and tougher penalties for agencies that fall out of compliance. And the election picture is firming up, with Democrats now holding at least fifty four Senate seats and two hundred twenty eight House seats, plus CVS announcing it will split into two companies. First, the physician fee schedule. CMS released the final rule for 2027 yesterday, and most of it takes effect January first. Start with remote monitoring, because it's closest to Resilient's world. Back in the summer, CMS proposed four changes to remote patient monitoring and remote therapeutic monitoring. Here's what happened to each. One, remote therapeutic monitoring will only be allowed for established patients. Finalized. Two, starting a patient on monitoring will now come with a separately billable initiating visit. Finalized. The CMS fact sheet doesn't say whether that visit has to be in person, so that's a detail to check in the full rule. Three, new payment values for the monitoring codes. Finalized, though the fact sheet doesn't list the new amounts. And four, the big one: paying for monitoring only when it's done by clinical staff employed by the practice, not by outside contractors. CMS finalized that, but delayed it two years, to January first, 2029. CMS also floated collapsing the monitoring codes into four new codes, and decided not to do that yet. It only collected comments for a future rule.

Why does the vendor ban matter? A large share of remote monitoring today is run by third party companies that supply the devices, watch the data and make the check in calls on behalf of a doctor's office. Becker's reports that Medicare spending on remote patient monitoring rose thirty one percent to five hundred thirty six million dollars in 2024, and CMS has been signaling that it thinks some of that growth is low value. The delay gives the vendor model a two year runway, but the direction is clear. By 2029, a practice that wants to bill Medicare for monitoring will need its own employed staff doing the work. That's my reading of the fact sheet language, and it's worth confirming against the rule text once your team reads it.

The rest of the rule. The base payment rate for most physicians drops two point three one percent, to about thirty two dollars and sixty three cents per unit. That's a deeper cut than proposed, mainly because a one time two and a half percent raise for 2026 expires. Doctors in advanced alternative payment models get a smaller cut, one point eight three percent. CMS also tilted payments toward accountable care organizations. A new billing modifier raises payment for office visits by sixteen percent, replacing an older add on code. A second modifier raises office visit payment by thirty two percent for doctors in the Medicare Shared Savings Program and the new LEAD accountable care model. And starting April first, 2027, approved accountable care organizations can reduce or eliminate patients' Part B cost sharing for many services. CMS says seventy five percent of four hundred seventy six accountable care organizations earned shared savings in 2024, totaling four point one billion dollars. Other pieces: new codes so clinical staff can do advance care planning under a doctor's supervision, payment pathways for health and well being coaching, separate billing for shared medical appointments, and the old MIPS quality program winding down by 2031. The press release quotes Health Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Junior saying Medicare should reward clinicians for keeping people healthy, and CMS Administrator Mehmet Oz saying fee for service has rewarded volume over value.

What it means for Resilient, and this is my analysis. Two things. First, the 2029 vendor ban is a strategic fork. If Resilient's plan involves being the outsourced monitoring service for practices, that model has a clock on it. If the plan is to sell technology and workflow that a practice's own staff use, the rule pushes customers toward you. Second, the money is moving to accountable care organizations. A thirty two percent office visit bump and permission to waive copays means these groups have more cash and more reasons to keep older patients out of the hospital. They're the buyers to court.

Second, home health. CMS also finalized the 2027 home health payment rule yesterday. Home Health Care News reports an overall two point four percent increase, which includes a temporary three percent cut CMS is applying to claw back what it considers past overpayments. The rule also lets CMS revoke an agency's Medicare enrollment back to the date its noncompliance began, rather than the date CMS caught it. CMS estimates that saves about eighty two million dollars a year. Doctor Oz said the changes will help protect seniors and safeguard taxpayer dollars. Industry groups had pushed for no cut at all, so expect continued lobbying in the new Congress. For agencies already paid less by Medicare Advantage, as we covered yesterday, a small raise doesn't change the squeeze.

Now the learning segment: longevity science and eldercare, and where they meet. These two worlds usually talk past each other. Longevity science, often called geroscience, tries to slow the biology of aging itself, so that heart disease, dementia, frailty and cancer all arrive later. Eldercare deals with people who already have those conditions and need help living with them. The money flows differently too. Longevity is funded mostly by venture capital, wealthy individuals and now government research agencies. Eldercare is funded mostly by Medicare, Medicaid and families' savings.

A few facts to anchor on. First, the gap. A Mayo Clinic study in JAMA, which we covered last week, found Americans live about twelve point four years in poor health at the end of life, the largest gap between lifespan and healthy lifespan among the countries studied. Second, the money is growing. Search results this week point to a new ARPA H program, the federal health research agency, awarding about one hundred forty four million dollars to seven teams testing whether already approved drugs can extend healthy lifespan. I couldn't open the article itself, so treat those details as unverified. There's also an XPrize for healthspan worth about one hundred one million dollars, and companies like Life Biosciences raising money for gene therapy trials aimed at aging. Third, the regulatory wall. From memory, the FDA doesn't recognize aging itself as a disease, so no drug can be approved simply for slowing aging. That's why the best known proposed trial, called TAME, which would test the diabetes drug metformin, is designed to measure whether several age related diseases arrive later, rather than aging itself.

So where do the two worlds meet? The most practical meeting point is a concept called intrinsic capacity, which the World Health Organization uses in a program called ICOPE. Intrinsic capacity means a person's physical and mental reserves: mobility, thinking, mood, vision, hearing and nutrition. It's measurable with simple tests like walking speed, a chair stand and a short memory screen. Decline in these measures predicts falls, hospitalization and needing long term care, often years ahead. And unlike the cellular biology that longevity companies chase, a lot of decline in intrinsic capacity responds to unglamorous things: strength training, protein, hearing aids, managing medications and treating depression. Senior living operators like Mather and HumanGood have started healthspan programs along these lines. My take is that for the next decade, most of the real gains for people over seventy five will come from this practical side, not from breakthrough anti aging drugs.

Today's opportunity, and it doesn't need any hardware. A healthspan measurement and coaching program sold to accountable care organizations and Medicare Advantage plans. Here's how it would work. Every six months, a trained coach or nurse runs a twenty minute intrinsic capacity check with each older member, in a clinic, in senior living, or partly by video: walking speed, chair stands, a memory screen, a hearing and vision check, mood and nutrition questions. People who are declining get a targeted plan, like strength classes, a hearing aid referral, a pharmacist medication review or a dietitian, with a coach following up by phone. Who pays: accountable care organizations and plans, per member per month, because declining capacity predicts the falls and hospital stays they pay for. Why now: yesterday's rule creates payment pathways for health and well being coaching and gives accountable care organizations bigger office visit payments and the ability to waive copays, so they can make it free to patients. Risks: the effect on costs takes time to show up, many seniors won't stick with exercise, and the evidence that screening alone improves outcomes is mixed, so the program has to deliver the follow through, not just the test. Simple wearables or phone based walking tests could make re checks cheaper later, but they aren't needed to start. The first test: partner with one Pittsburgh area accountable care organization or senior living community, check two hundred people, and see after a year whether the people flagged as declining are the ones who fell or went to the hospital, and whether the coaching changed it.

A question to think about: if Medicare is paying for coaching and keeping people healthy, who will decide what counts as a healthspan result worth paying for, and could a simple measure like walking speed become the number everyone gets paid on? That's your briefing. Have a good Thursday.
