First, the money. Two deals this week, both with CVS Health's venture arm involved. Abby Care closed an eighty two million dollar Series C, led by Transformation Capital. Abby recruits, trains, pays and supervises family members as paid caregivers through Medicaid, with nurses overseeing the care. Baba raised a forty million dollar Series A, led by FirstMark Capital. Baba does Medicare focused care navigation and care management, using patient advocates and AI to run its operations, and it says it was accepted into all four clinical tracks of a new CMS payment model called ACCESS. That model, as I understand it, pays for technology enabled management of chronic conditions, but I'm working from memory there, so it's worth a closer look. Why this matters: the two most funded ideas in aging care right now are paying families to do care they already do, and helping people find their way through Medicare. Neither is a device. And CVS keeps placing bets on care at home, which tells you where a big pharmacy and insurance company thinks the money is going.

Second, nursing home quality. The Boston Globe's Spotlight team, republished by STAT, looked at nursing home chains that grew quickly in Massachusetts, including RegalCare, run by an operator who came in from New Jersey. One one hundred bed home in Taunton had the top five star federal rating for more than two years. After the new owner took over, the paper reports, care was cut sharply, and millions of dollars flowed to related real estate and management companies owned by the same people, with little action from the state. Most of the story is behind a paywall, so I only saw the opening. At the same time, CMS has started a new risk based survey program. Nursing homes with five stars, clean recent inspections and no recent change of owner get less frequent inspections and a gold trophy next to their name on Medicare's comparison website. CMS expected about twelve percent of homes to qualify. But the advocacy group Consumer Voice looked at past data and found that roughly half of homes that would have earned the label lost eligibility a year later. Why this matters: quality in nursing homes can fall apart fast, especially after ownership changes, and the official ratings lag behind. Families and referral sources need better early warning signals than a star rating.

Third, medications and Medicare's star ratings. CMS released the 2027 star ratings for Medicare Advantage and drug plans on October eighth. About thirty seven percent of Medicare Advantage drug plan contracts got four stars or more, but those contracts hold about seventy one percent of enrollees, and fifteen contracts got five stars. The average rating slipped slightly, to three point nine nine. Two new measures were added: one penalizes plans when members take opioids and benzodiazepines together, and one penalizes plans when older members take several anticholinergic drugs at once. Those are drugs like some sleep aids, bladder medications and older antidepressants that can cause confusion and falls in older people. Medication adherence measures, for diabetes, blood pressure and cholesterol drugs, already count triple. And adherence to diabetes drugs got worse this year, with the average Medicare Advantage score dropping from three point one to two point seven stars. Why this matters: star ratings drive billions of dollars in bonus payments, so insurers will now pay for help getting members to take the right drugs and stop the wrong ones.

Today's learning segment is medication adherence and polypharmacy. Two problems that pull in opposite directions. Adherence is whether people take the drugs they're prescribed, the right dose at the right time. Polypharmacy means taking a lot of drugs, usually defined as five or more. Older adults have both problems at once: they skip drugs that matter, and they take drugs that no longer help, or that cause harm. Here's the scale, from a 2019 Lown Institute report, so these are older numbers. About forty two percent of Americans sixty five and older took five or more prescription drugs, up from about fourteen percent in the mid nineties. Counting over the counter drugs and supplements, about two thirds did, and nearly one in five took ten or more. The report estimated that adverse drug events led to about two hundred eighty thousand hospital stays a year among older adults, and that about one in five older people had a drug related harm each year. The causes are simple. People see many specialists, each adding drugs, and nobody owns the whole list. Drugs started for a short problem never get stopped. And side effects get treated with another drug, which is called a prescribing cascade. Certain drug classes cause most of the trouble in older people: blood thinners, diabetes drugs that can push blood sugar too low, sedatives and sleeping pills, opioids, and anticholinergic drugs. The American Geriatrics Society keeps a list of drugs to avoid in older people, called the Beers Criteria.

On adherence, the reasons people don't take drugs are mostly cost, side effects, confusion over complex schedules, memory problems, and not believing the drug helps. Fixes include simplifying regimens, ninety day refills and mail order, pill packs sorted by time of day, pharmacist reviews, and follow up calls. Medicare requires drug plans to offer a yearly comprehensive medication review for people with several chronic conditions and high drug costs, usually done by a pharmacist.

Now the honest part on evidence. A large review in JAMA Network Open in 2025 looked at one hundred eighteen randomized trials covering more than four hundred thousand older adults. Interventions like medication reviews and decision support reduced the number of drugs by about half a drug per person. But they made no clear difference to falls, adverse drug reactions, hospital stays, quality of life or deaths. So deprescribing programs reliably remove pills, but haven't yet proven they make people healthier. That's either a reason for doubt, or an opening for someone who can design programs and studies that show real outcomes.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: a pharmacist led medication review and deprescribing service for Medicare Advantage plans and risk bearing primary care groups, starting in western Pennsylvania. The customer is a Medicare Advantage plan or a value based primary care group responsible for the total cost of care for its older patients. The problem: new star measures now penalize risky drug combinations, adherence measures count triple, diabetes adherence is slipping, and drug harms drive avoidable hospital stays. The service would use claims and pharmacy data to find the members at highest risk, have clinical pharmacists, mostly by phone and video, review their full medication lists, work with their doctors to stop or switch risky drugs, simplify schedules and fix cost barriers. Who pays: the plan or group, per member reviewed or per month, with part of the fee tied to improvement in star measures and avoided hospital stays. Why now: the new measures, the bonus money tied to them, and plans looking for ways to cut costs after pulling out of markets. The risks: established competitors already sell adherence services to plans, doctors can resist changes suggested by outside pharmacists, and as the review showed, proving health benefits is hard. The edge would come from running it as a real study from the start, with a comparison group and outcome tracking, which is where Resilient's clinical research experience fits. Pittsburgh also has a strong pharmacy school with a geriatrics focus, though that's from memory. No hardware is needed. Smart pill dispensers exist, but they're optional, and only worth it for the small group with memory problems who live alone. The first test is to ask two Pennsylvania Medicare Advantage plans or primary care groups what they currently pay for adherence and medication review, and whether the new anticholinergic measure is on their list of problems for next year.

A question to think about this Sunday: if half a pill less per person doesn't change outcomes, what would a medication program have to do differently to actually keep someone out of the hospital? That's your briefing. Enjoy your Sunday.
