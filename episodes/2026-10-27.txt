Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Tuesday, October twenty seventh. Three things today, and the first one is directly about Resilient's world. KFF Health News has a major story on Medicare's proposed remote monitoring rule, saying it could virtually wipe out the outside vendors that run most of these programs. The country's largest hospital at home company, DispatchHealth, has a new chief executive and is cutting back to become a behind the scenes partner for health systems. And insurers are suing Medicare over star ratings, while eighty percent of hospitals are now paying readmission penalties. Today's learning segment is hospital readmissions and care transitions, the dangerous days after an older person leaves the hospital.

First, remote monitoring. KFF Health News reported yesterday that the proposed Medicare physician payment rule would stop paying for remote patient monitoring when an outside technology vendor delivers the service. Clinicians would only be paid when someone at their own practice does the work. CMS points to Inspector General reports from 2024 and 2025 that found fraud risks, poor data collection, and patients who weren't being managed by the doctor who ordered the device. The numbers show why the rule matters but also why CMS is nervous. Only about one percent of traditional Medicare patients use remote monitoring, but enrollment grew tenfold between 2019 and 2023, and spending went from under seven million dollars to more than one hundred ninety four million. Caroline Pearson of the Peterson Center on Healthcare said the rule could virtually eliminate remote monitoring, with rural patients hit hardest, and a rural clinic network in Georgia said it couldn't offer monitoring at all without a vendor. More than two hundred thirty companies, health systems and advocacy groups signed a letter opposing it. But Humana, the Blue Cross Blue Shield Association and Sutter Health largely support it. Pearson suggested limits on how long monitoring can run, more outcome data, and payment tied to results, and she noted that CMS's new ACCESS model does pay vendors, but at lower rates. The final rule is expected any day now, usually around the first of November. Why this matters: if the rule goes through as proposed, the business model where a monitoring company runs the program for a doctor's office in traditional Medicare stops working. What survives is monitoring run by practices' own staff, by hospital at home programs, by Medicare Advantage plans, or under outcome based models like ACCESS. That's the clearest signal yet that devices alone, sold through fee for service billing, are a fragile business.

Second, DispatchHealth. Home Health Care News reports that DispatchHealth, which merged with Medically Home last year to become the largest hospital at home provider in the country, is naming its president, Erin Bartley, as chief executive on November first. The company is ending its full service emergency room alternative model that it sold to insurers, ending relationships with six health systems, and laying off an undisclosed number of workers. Bartley said insurers aren't investing the way they used to, and that providers now have to prove complex home care saves money. The new plan is to become what she called the infrastructure layer for complex care at home, supporting health systems rather than competing with them. About forty percent of its customers are technology only deals, and about sixty percent use it to manage networks of contracted community providers. Why this matters: this confirms yesterday's opportunity. Health systems want help with the logistics of home based care, and the market leader is pivoting to exactly that. It also means a competitor with scale is now going after the same job, so a smaller player would need a sharp local or specialty niche.

Third, quality ratings and penalties. Centene sued CMS in federal court in Georgia over its 2027 Medicare Advantage star ratings, challenging seventeen measures and saying it stands to lose about four hundred forty eight million dollars in bonus payments and rebates. It's the latest of several insurers to sue, after Alignment Healthcare, Healthfirst and Clover Health, which won a ruling this spring. Separately, CMS is funding a two hundred seventy five million dollar program with states to attract nurses to nursing homes in short staffed areas, offering up to forty thousand dollars in student loan repayment or a ten thousand dollar stipend for a three year commitment. I only saw a summary of that one. And on hospitals, Healthcare Dive reports that for the fiscal year that started October first, about eighty percent of hospitals, two thousand three hundred thirty four of them, are paying Medicare readmission penalties, and for the first time the penalties include data on Medicare Advantage patients. Most penalties are under one percent of Medicare payments, with ten hospitals at the three percent maximum. Why this matters: hospitals still have a direct financial reason to cut readmissions, and now their Medicare Advantage patients count too.

On the business side, Devoted Health is expanding its Medicare Advantage plans into more rural New Hampshire counties, a small sign that some insurers are still growing even as Humana and UnitedHealthcare pull back. On the social side, I couldn't reach Reddit or Hacker News directly, so nothing reliable to report.

The Resilient angle. Today's remote monitoring story is the most important thing for Resilient this month. If the rule is finalized as proposed, selling a device plus a monitoring service to physician practices in traditional Medicare becomes very hard. The paths that remain are selling through organizations that carry risk or employ their own staff: hospital at home programs, Medicare Advantage plans, risk bearing primary care groups, and outcome based models like ACCESS. All of those buy outcomes, not readings. So the question for Resilient is less about which device features to build and more about which customer pays for a result and how to prove that result. Today's opportunity is one answer that doesn't need a device at all.

Today's learning segment is hospital readmissions and care transitions. A care transition is any move between settings: hospital to home, hospital to nursing facility, nursing facility to home. For older adults these moments are dangerous. Medication lists change, follow up appointments don't happen, test results come back after discharge with nobody watching, and family caregivers are sent home with instructions they didn't fully understand. Roughly one in six or seven Medicare patients is readmitted within thirty days, a figure I'm quoting from memory, and studies have long estimated that a meaningful share of those readmissions is preventable. Medicare's Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program, created by the Affordable Care Act, penalizes hospitals with higher than expected readmissions for a set of conditions, including heart attack, heart failure, pneumonia, COPD, bypass surgery and hip and knee replacement, by up to three percent of their Medicare payments. The program did lower readmissions after it started, though researchers have argued about how much was real and how much was coding and observation stays, and it penalizes safety net hospitals more often because their patients are poorer and sicker. What works? The best known models are the Care Transitions Intervention developed by Eric Coleman, which uses a coach to help patients and caregivers manage medications, recognize warning signs and get to follow up; Project RED at Boston Medical Center, a standard discharge checklist; and the Transitional Care Model from the University of Pennsylvania, where advanced practice nurses follow high risk older patients from hospital into the home. The common ingredients are medication reconciliation, a follow up visit within about a week, a named person the patient can call, and coaching the family caregiver. Medicare also pays primary care practices for transitional care management, which requires contact with the patient within two business days of discharge and a visit within seven or fourteen days depending on complexity. Those billing details are from memory, so check them. Where does remote monitoring fit? For heart failure, the big trials of monitoring after discharge have mostly been disappointing, so it's not a magic fix. The people and the process matter more than the device.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: a hospital to home transition service for hospitals and risk bearing primary care groups in western Pennsylvania. The customer is a hospital facing readmission penalties, now including its Medicare Advantage patients, and value based primary care groups or Medicare Advantage plans that pay for those readmissions. The problem: the first thirty days after discharge are where older patients fall through the cracks, and most hospitals and practices don't have enough staff to follow every high risk patient home. The service would identify high risk older patients before discharge, have a nurse or trained coach contact them within forty eight hours, do a home visit with full medication reconciliation in the first week, make sure the follow up visit happens, and coach the family caregiver, using the evidence based models I just described. Who pays: hospitals per patient enrolled, with part of the fee tied to readmission reductions, and primary care groups that can bill transitional care management if the staffing is structured to meet Medicare's rules. Why now: eighty percent of hospitals are being penalized, Medicare Advantage patients now count, and if the remote monitoring rule goes through, practices will need human centered transition programs that don't depend on outside device vendors. Risks: hospitals have tried transition programs before with mixed results, many have in house teams, and staffing nurses and coaches is expensive. The edge would be running it as a measured program from day one, with a comparison group, so you can prove readmissions actually fell. Hardware isn't needed. Short term monitoring could be an option for a few patients, but the trial evidence for monitoring after discharge is weak, so it shouldn't be the core. The first test is to ask two western Pennsylvania hospitals what their readmission penalty is this year, which patients drive it, and what they already do in the first week after discharge.

A question to think about today: if Medicare stops paying outside vendors for monitoring, who becomes the natural buyer of a monitoring device, and what would they need to see before they'd pay for it? That's your briefing. Have a good Tuesday.
