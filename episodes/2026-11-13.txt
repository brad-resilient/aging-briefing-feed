Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Friday, November thirteenth. The big news first: CMS extended the freeze on new hospices and home health agencies for another six months, through May twelfth of next year, and kept it nationwide. That's not what I expected, so I'll say plainly where my forecast went wrong. Second, more health systems are getting out of the insurance business: about ten of the eighteen companies leaving Medicare Advantage next year are owned at least partly by health systems. Third, nursing home staffing is slowly growing again, but one state shows how quality can slip at the same time. Today's learning segment follows from the second story: provider-sponsored health plans, why so many are closing, and what the integrated models that work do differently.

First, the freeze. On Thursday afternoon CMS filed two notices with the Federal Register, one for hospices and one for home health agencies, extending both enrollment moratoria for another six months. They took effect Thursday, November twelfth, and run through Wednesday, May twelfth, 2027. The hospice notice is scheduled to be formally published on Monday. The freeze still covers the whole country, but it's a bit narrower than before. It now applies only to new agencies enrolling for the first time, including certain ownership changes that count as new enrollments. Existing hospices can once again add new practice locations, and the home health side, according to a summary from the accrediting body CHAP, no longer blocks new branches either. I read the hospice notice myself; the home health details come from that summary.

Why CMS did it. The hospice notice cites the inspector general saying there are significant problems with the hospice program. It says Nevada's number of hospices grew one hundred fifty one percent between 2019 and 2023. In a special review of about six hundred seventy hospices in Arizona, California, Nevada and Texas, CMS revoked one hundred twenty two, an eighteen percent revocation rate compared with about one to three percent normally. It mentions an FBI alert on hospice fraud from June, and recent criminal cases. CMS says it looked at limiting the freeze to southern California and rejected that because the problems are nationwide. And it says it found no evidence that the freeze has materially reduced patients' access to hospice, nationally or in any state. Dr. Oz said fraud in hospice and home health persists and the work isn't finished. Industry groups again called the approach overly broad.

Where I was wrong. For the past week I said my expectation was a narrower extension aimed at high fraud states. CMS considered exactly that and rejected it. The lesson for me is that the fraud data CMS has, especially that eighteen percent revocation rate, is stronger than the industry's access argument, at least for now. The lesson for anyone thinking about this market: for at least the next six months, the only way into Medicare hospice or home health is to buy or partner with an agency that's already enrolled, and even some ownership changes are caught by the freeze. Existing licenses just got more valuable, and buyers will need to be sure the agency they're buying is clean. Aveanna said this week that hospice acquisitions are a priority, and smaller deals keep happening, like Davian buying Healthcare Stat for thirteen and a half million dollars.

Second, health systems leaving Medicare Advantage. Becker's counted eighteen insurers exiting Medicare Advantage for 2027 using CMS data, and classifies ten of them as wholly or partly owned by health systems. The list includes Providence Health Plan, UMass Memorial's Mass Advantage, Memorial Hermann in Texas, Trinity Health's Saint Alphonsus plan in Idaho, Boston Medical Center's WellSense, Christus, Sharp, and joint ventures like CarePartners of Connecticut and Braven Health in New Jersey. Earlier this year, Carle Health and Michigan Medicine also wound down plans. Providence's plan, which covers about four hundred forty thousand people across all its lines, lost a hundred two million dollars last year, blaming higher medical and drug use and a drop in star ratings. Meanwhile, Becker's now counts at least eleven insurers suing CMS over this year's star ratings, saying the ratings will cost them nearly four billion dollars combined, and several of those are health system plans, including Banner, Trinity's MediGold and Jefferson Health Plans. And going the other way, MultiCare in Washington State just launched a Medicare Advantage plan anyway, aiming for a couple thousand members, because its finance chief says traditional Medicare pays too little and fighting big insurers over prior authorization is too costly.

Third, nursing home workforce and quality. Skilled Nursing News reports that federal jobs data show nursing and residential care employment reached about three and a half million in October, and skilled nursing facilities added about two thousand one hundred jobs that month, and are about thirty three thousand higher than a year ago. Operators credit international recruitment, nontraditional labor pools and growing their own leaders, though international nurses take two to three years to arrive and aides about five. At the same time, the Minneapolis Star Tribune found that one star nursing homes in Minnesota rose from thirty four in 2019 to sixty four now, federal fines tripled, more than thirty homes closed or converted to assisted living, and eighty four facilities had to replace at least half their nursing staff in the past year. Minnesota's nursing home associations are asking for a guarantee that every resident has a nursing home within forty five minutes of home. More staff overall doesn't mean stable staff in any one building.

A few more items. The nonprofit Ebenezer, the senior living arm of Fairview Health in Minnesota, is buying a South Dakota management company and taking over eight communities. Kaplan Senior Living says it's going after singles and doubles rather than home run acquisitions. The Center to Advance Palliative Care is building a national inventory of palliative care programs. And Senior Housing News used the collapse of a cruise ship sold as a floating retirement home, seized in Singapore this month after selling cabins from ninety nine thousand dollars, as a warning that senior living shouldn't be sold as a permanent vacation.

Now the learning segment: provider-sponsored health plans. These are insurance plans owned by a hospital system, sometimes called payviders. The idea is appealing. If the same organization collects the premium and delivers the care, it should be able to cut waste, keep people healthier, and keep the savings instead of handing them to an outside insurer. Big examples that work include Kaiser Permanente, and, from memory, Geisinger and Pittsburgh's own UPMC Health Plan, which is one of the largest provider-owned plans in the country.

So why are so many closing? Four reasons come up again and again. One, scale. Insurance has big fixed costs: claims systems, compliance, actuaries, star ratings programs, coding for risk adjustment. A plan with fifteen or sixty thousand members spreads those costs over far too few people, while national insurers have millions. Two, the market got harder. Since 2024, medical use by older adults rose faster than expected, CMS tightened rate updates and changed how risk scores are calculated, and star rating cut points moved. Three, conflicting incentives. A health system's hospitals earn money from more admissions and procedures, while its plan earns money from fewer. Unless leadership truly runs the whole thing as one budget, the hospital side usually wins internal arguments. Four, members don't stay put. Many go to doctors outside the system, so the plan pays other hospitals full price and gets little of the integration benefit.

What the winners do differently. Kaiser is the extreme case: it owns the plan, employs its doctors through its medical groups, and most members get nearly all their care inside the system, so the incentives truly line up. Devoted Health is the opposite model: a tech-driven insurer with its own clinical teams but no hospitals. Devoted says it grew to over four hundred sixty six thousand members in January, up a hundred twenty one percent in a year, across twenty nine states, raised about three hundred sixty six million dollars in new equity, and has nearly all members in four star plans. The common thread is not hospital ownership. It's tight control over the member experience, strong data and coding, and a care model that keeps people out of the hospital.

What it means for Resilient, as my analysis. Small plans that stay in the market, and health systems that left but still carry risk through contracts, can't afford to build every program in house. They buy services that move stars and keep members out of hospitals, but each one has to justify itself in dollars.

Today's opportunity, and it's not a device: a billing integrity and quality review service for hospice and home health deals. With the freeze extended, the only way to enter or grow in these markets is to buy existing agencies, and CMS is revoking problem hospices at an eighteen percent rate in the targeted states. Buyers, lenders and private equity firms need to know before they close whether an agency's patients actually qualify, whether lengths of stay and live discharges look normal, whether its records support its billing, and whether it might be caught in a revocation. The service would combine clinical chart review by hospice nurses and physicians with analysis of the agency's billing patterns against national norms, and would deliver a risk rating and a clean up plan. Who pays: acquirers, lenders and investors, per deal, and agencies preparing for sale or audit. Why now: licenses are scarce, the freeze runs to May, hospice M&A is a stated priority for companies like Aveanna, and enforcement is sharp. Risks: established consulting and law firms already do diligence, the work depends on experienced clinicians, and if the freeze ends, urgency drops. Resilient's fit is its clinical study and regulatory background, not its hardware, and no device is needed. A first test: offer chart and billing reviews to two or three Pennsylvania or Ohio deal teams, both states that have their own state hospice freezes, and see whether they'll pay a fixed fee per deal.

A question to think about: if the fastest way into a frozen market is to buy an existing license, who protects patients from the agencies that were built mainly to be sold? That's your briefing. Have a good weekend.
