Good morning Brad. This is your aging and elder care briefing for Monday, October nineteenth. Three things to start the week. Medicare open enrollment is underway, and for many older adults it's a year of shrinking choices and rising drug costs. A program called PACE, which keeps nursing home eligible seniors living at home, is having a moment, with a bill in Congress and a public company posting record profits. And a new study says new dementia cases worldwide will more than double by 2050, as a major prevention trial starts in Colombia. Today's learning segment is PACE itself: how it works, how it gets paid, and why it's growing.

First, Medicare open enrollment. It started last Thursday and runs through December seventh, with changes taking effect January first. The headlines for 2027: the annual cap on out of pocket drug costs in Part D rises to two thousand four hundred dollars, up from two thousand one hundred, and the standard drug deductible rises to seven hundred dollars. Medicare's negotiated prices kick in for fifteen drugs, including Ozempic and Wegovy, which the government says will save enrollees about six hundred eighty five million dollars next year. Meanwhile, some Medicare Advantage plans are leaving counties and cutting extras like dental. The White House also announced a one time ninety dollar payment toward Part B premiums for more than twenty million people in traditional Medicare, which doesn't reach the roughly thirty five million in Medicare Advantage. Why this matters: every fall, millions of older adults and their adult children try to make sense of plan changes, and this year more of them are being forced to switch. Confusion is a business. Brokers, navigation services and plan comparison tools all live off this season, and the plans themselves are pickier about which members and providers they want.

Second, PACE is having a moment. PACE stands for Program of All inclusive Care for the Elderly. In late September, Representatives Debbie Dingell, a Democrat, and John Moolenaar, a Republican, introduced the PACE Access Improvement Act. It would make PACE a required Medicaid benefit in every state instead of optional, let people enroll on any day of the month instead of waiting for the first, and loosen marketing and expansion limits. LeadingAge, which supports the bill, says passage this year is unlikely, so think of it as a signal, not a done deal. On the business side, InnovAge, the largest publicly traded PACE operator, reported its fiscal year in September: revenue up about sixteen percent to nearly nine hundred ninety million dollars, adjusted earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization nearly tripled to about ninety five million, and about eight thousand two hundred thirty participants. Do the division and that's roughly one hundred twenty thousand dollars of revenue per participant per year. And in California, which has about a third of all PACE enrollees nationally, enrollment has tripled since 2020, and for profit programs, including ones backed by private equity and venture capital, now serve about forty percent of enrollees. California actually paused new PACE applications in late 2025 because growth was so fast.

Third, dementia. A study in JAMA Neurology, pooling twenty two studies of more than thirty eight thousand people in eighteen countries, estimates that about eleven million people developed dementia in 2023, and that new cases each year will reach nearly twenty five million by 2050, more than double today. Women and people in lower income countries are at higher risk. In the same week, the Banner Alzheimer's Institute and partners in Colombia randomized the first participant in a new prevention trial in a large family that carries a gene mutation causing early Alzheimer's, usually in their mid forties. It's testing Lilly's donanemab, which clears amyloid plaques, and an experimental Roche pill meant to stop plaques from coming back, alone and together, with about seventy four and a half million dollars from the National Institute on Aging. Results are years away, but the direction is clear: treatment is moving earlier, toward people with no symptoms, which means more testing, more monitoring and longer care relationships.

On policy, two quick items. Last week, the federal Administration for Community Living awarded up to twenty seven point eight million dollars over three years to scale falls prevention programs, mainly the CDC's STEADI screening approach and the Stepping On community program, through the National Council on Aging and eight state and local groups including Maine, Maryland, New York and Texas. Remember yesterday's falls trial: simple programs work, and now there's public money to spread them. And in New Hampshire, the state settled a class action brought by AARP Foundation and disability advocates who said the state's Medicaid home care program was so understaffed that people were at risk of being forced into nursing homes. The state agreed to measure how many people are missing their authorized services, publish reports twice a year, review pay rates, and ask the legislature for more money. That's notable: "authorized but not delivered" hours are becoming something states must count and report.

In tech and business, a few signals. Electronic Caregiver now lets private duty home care agencies put their own brand on its virtual caregiver, Addison, so agencies can sell a tech layer between visits. A company called Ashvin raised sixteen and a half million dollars to automate paperwork for medical equipment suppliers. And a new platform called ValuTrak aims to help non medical home care agencies measure outcomes, which matters because payers increasingly want proof, not just hours.

The Resilient angle. The common thread today is integrated, paid for outcome care at home. PACE, Medicare Advantage special needs plans and home based primary care all get a fixed amount per person and win by keeping people out of hospitals and nursing homes. Those are customers who'll pay for anything that demonstrably does that, whether it's a service, staffing, software, or occasionally a device. Pennsylvania is a good place to learn this, because its PACE programs, called LIFE here, already include about thirteen providers and around fifty eight sites, including LIFE Pittsburgh and InnovAge.

Today's learning segment is PACE. PACE is for people fifty five and older who qualify for nursing home level care but can live safely in the community with support, and who live in a PACE service area. Most, about eighty two percent nationally, are eligible for both Medicare and Medicaid. Here's the model. A PACE organization takes full responsibility for all of a person's care, everything from doctor visits and drugs to home care, transportation, hospital stays and, if needed, nursing home care. In return it receives one combined monthly payment from Medicare and Medicaid per person. Most care is organized around a day center, where participants come a few days a week for meals, therapy, activities and clinic visits, and an interdisciplinary team, doctor, nurses, social worker, therapists, aides and drivers, meets constantly to manage each person. Because the PACE organization pays for hospital stays, it has every reason to prevent them. The results are good: research links PACE to fewer hospitalizations and lower mortality than similar people outside it, and people rarely leave voluntarily. Nationally, PACE serves more than ninety six thousand people in thirty three states plus Washington DC. That's tiny compared with the millions who'd qualify. Why so small? Three reasons. Starting a program is slow and expensive, with a new center costing millions and years of state and federal approvals before break even. Enrollment is hard, because people must give up their own doctors and many families have never heard of it. And the day center model struggles in rural areas: in California, ninety four percent of PACE sites are in cities. Since 2016, for profit companies have been allowed in, and private equity and venture capital have followed, because a full PACE program generates roughly one hundred twenty thousand dollars per participant per year, and well run centers are profitable. The open questions are quality at fast growing for profit programs, and whether PACE can work with fewer centers and more care delivered at home.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: an enrollment and growth partner for PACE programs in Pennsylvania. Every PACE organization's main constraint is census, the number of enrolled participants, and the funnel is slow: referral, home visit, Medicaid financial eligibility, a nursing home level of care assessment, then enrollment only on the first of the month. Many interested families drop out along the way. The idea is a service that runs that funnel for LIFE programs: building referral relationships with hospital discharge planners, Area Agencies on Aging and primary care practices, guiding families through the Medicaid paperwork, and tracking every referral so none go cold. Each additional participant is worth on the order of a hundred thousand dollars a year to the program, so even a few more enrollments a month matter. One important caution: paying or being paid per enrollee in a Medicare and Medicaid program can run into federal anti kickback and PACE marketing rules, so this would need a flat fee structure and a healthcare lawyer from day one. Other risks: programs may prefer to build this in house, and growth is limited by center capacity and staffing. Hardware isn't needed at all. Resilient's fit is its Pittsburgh base, its comfort with regulated healthcare and compliance, and the chance to learn how capitated organizations buy. The first test is to call three western Pennsylvania LIFE programs and ask about their census, their waitlist, how long it takes from referral to enrollment, and where families drop out.

A question to think about: if a program can earn a hundred and twenty thousand dollars a year for keeping one frail older person safely at home, what would you build to keep ten more people at home? That's your briefing. Have a good Monday.
