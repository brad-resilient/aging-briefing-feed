Alzheimer's blood tests are spreading faster than the rules around them, and a JAMA essay warns about turning healthy people into patients in waiting. Medicare's new enforcement power has already been used, against adult day care centers in New York City. And the new national numbers on dementia show just how big the care burden has become, including here in Pennsylvania. Today's learning segment is dementia and Alzheimer's: how it's diagnosed, what the new drugs do and don't do, and what caregivers actually face.

First, Alzheimer's blood tests. In a JAMA viewpoint published Monday, neurologist Gayatri Devi argues that the real question isn't how accurate the new blood tests are, but whether people without symptoms should be tested at all. The first blood test, Fujirebio's Lumipulse, was cleared by the FDA in May 2025 for adults fifty five and older who already show signs of cognitive decline, and the FDA said it's not meant for screening. In people with symptoms, about ninety two percent of positive results matched brain scans or spinal fluid tests. But many people with Alzheimer's changes in the brain never develop dementia, there's no approved treatment for people without symptoms, and the federal genetic nondiscrimination law doesn't cover these tests, and doesn't apply to life, disability or long term care insurance at all. So a positive result in a medical record could, depending on state rules, affect someone's ability to buy long term care insurance. Meanwhile research is moving fast. In the UK, a new adaptive platform trial called AD SMART plans to enroll twelve hundred people to test two repurposed drugs, one used for type two diabetes and one for ADHD, dropping drugs that fail and adding new ones as it goes. Why this matters: more people will learn their Alzheimer's risk years before symptoms, and they'll need help deciding what to do with that information. That's a services gap, not a technology gap.

Second, enforcement. Last week we reported that CMS gave itself the power to remove providers from Medicare and Medicaid without going through the Inspector General. STAT reports that one week later, federal officials used it to target adult day care centers in New York City over alleged Medicaid fraud. Adult day programs, especially so called social adult day care, have been a long running fraud concern in New York. Put this alongside the hospice and home health enrollment freeze, the Medicaid payment suspensions, and the GUIDE model paying for adult day respite, and there's a clear message: legitimate adult day and home based services are needed more than ever, but the bar for proving they're real has gone way up. Separately, a fire destroyed much of Brookdale's assisted living community in Clinton, Mississippi, which housed more than a hundred people, many with limited mobility. Everyone was evacuated and there were no confirmed casualties, a reminder of how much emergency planning matters in buildings full of frail residents.

Third, the numbers. The Alzheimer's Association's 2026 report estimates seven point four million Americans sixty five and older are living with Alzheimer's, about three quarters of them seventy five or older. Nearly thirteen million family members and friends provide unpaid care, more than nineteen billion hours last year, worth more than four hundred forty six billion dollars. Paid health and long term care for people with dementia costs about four hundred nine billion dollars a year, with Medicare and Medicaid covering about sixty four percent and families paying about one hundred three billion out of pocket. In Pennsylvania, about two hundred eighty two thousand older adults have Alzheimer's, cared for by about four hundred seventy two thousand unpaid caregivers, whose work is valued at about seventeen billion dollars. And the state has only about four hundred fifty five geriatricians. One more research note: the Washington Post reports researchers examining the brains of eleven people who died in their eighties and nineties found air pollution particles that had reached deep into the brain, including areas tied to memory. It's a tiny study, but it adds to evidence linking pollution and dementia.

The Resilient angle. Three thoughts. First, dementia is the single biggest cost driver in elder care, and most of that cost is unpaid family labor. Businesses that reduce that burden or help families plan have a huge market. Second, Resilient's clinical trial experience is directly relevant: Alzheimer's research is shifting to prevention trials that need to find, screen and keep thousands of healthy people at risk, and recruitment is one of the most expensive and slowest parts of those trials. Third, devices have a limited role in dementia care today, mostly in wandering and safety, and families often resist them. The bigger needs are navigation, planning and caregiver support.

Today's learning segment is dementia and Alzheimer's. Dementia is the umbrella term for decline in memory and thinking serious enough to interfere with daily life. Alzheimer's disease causes most cases, with vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia and frontotemporal dementia making up most of the rest, and many people have more than one. Diagnosis traditionally meant a clinical evaluation, cognitive testing and ruling out other causes, and many people are never formally diagnosed, or are diagnosed late. Confirming Alzheimer's specifically used to require an amyloid PET scan or a spinal tap. Blood tests now make that cheaper and easier for people with symptoms. On treatment, two antibody drugs, lecanemab, sold as Leqembi, and donanemab, sold as Kisunla, remove amyloid plaques and are approved for early stage Alzheimer's. In their main trials they slowed decline by roughly a quarter to a third over about eighteen months, a real but modest effect. They require regular infusions, repeated MRI scans to watch for brain swelling and small bleeds, which can be serious, and a confirmed amyloid diagnosis. Those trial figures are from memory, so check them before quoting. That's why so much research is moving to prevention, treating people before symptoms. Now the care burden. Most people with dementia live at home, cared for by family, often a spouse or adult daughter, for years. Behavioral symptoms, like agitation, sleep disruption and suspicion, are often harder for families than memory loss, and they're a major reason people move to memory care. Wandering is a big safety concern: the Alzheimer's Association has long said that a majority of people with dementia will wander at some point, and getting lost can be dangerous. Solutions range from simple door alarms and identification bracelets to GPS devices, but the most important factor is a caregiver who knows the risk and has a plan. Finally, where the money comes from: Medicare pays for medical care, diagnosis and, now through GUIDE, some care coordination and respite; Medicaid pays for most long term nursing home and home and community care for people who qualify; and families pay most everything else, in money and time.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: an Alzheimer's prevention trial recruitment and navigation service, starting in Pennsylvania. As blood tests spread, more people in their fifties and sixties will learn they have Alzheimer's changes in the brain with no symptoms and no approved treatment. Meanwhile, drug companies and academic groups are launching prevention trials that need exactly those people. The idea is a service that helps at risk adults understand their results and options, connects them with brain health and planning resources, and, for those who want it, matches and screens them for appropriate prevention trials. The trial sponsors and research sites pay for recruitment and screening, which is standard practice in clinical research, and families get free, independent navigation. Why now: blood testing is expanding, prevention trials are growing, and memory clinics are overloaded. Risks: privacy and the insurance questions raised in the JAMA essay must be handled carefully and honestly; sponsors may prefer large national recruiters; and any payment from sponsors must not bias the advice families get. Hardware isn't needed. Resilient's fit is strong: it has run clinical studies, understands consent, data and regulators, and is based in Pittsburgh, home to major academic Alzheimer's research. The first test is to talk to two Alzheimer's trial sites in Pittsburgh and one sponsor about their recruitment costs, screen failure rates, and how hard it is to find people with positive biomarkers but no symptoms.
