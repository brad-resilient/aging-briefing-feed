Today's learning topic is wearables for older adults, so this edition leans into devices for once, honestly. Three things. While we wait for Medicare's final remote monitoring rule, it's worth being precise about what was proposed, because it would change how monitoring devices get paid for. A startup called Kivo Health raised ten million dollars for virtual lung rehab at home, and it shows what a device business that works actually looks like. And a bipartisan bill to grow PACE, the all inclusive care program for frail seniors, now has more detail and a Senate version coming next month.

First, the remote monitoring rule, in detail. The final physician payment rule is expected around the first of November. Here's what CMS proposed in July, according to the nursing home trade group's summary. Remote monitoring could only be billed for clinical staff employed by the billing practice, which effectively bars outside vendors. Patients would need an initial face to face visit before monitoring starts. Remote therapeutic monitoring would be limited to established patients. And CMS asked for comments on replacing the seventeen current remote monitoring billing codes with four bundled codes. That last part matters for any revenue model, because bundled codes usually mean less money for the device portion and more emphasis on the clinical work. Why this matters: if most of this survives into the final rule, the value of a monitoring device in traditional Medicare will depend on a practice choosing to run the program itself. That puts pressure on devices to be simple for clinic staff, reliable in the data they send, and tied to a clinical result.

Second, Kivo Health. Home Health Care News reports that Kivo, a Seattle company, raised a ten million dollar Series A led by Allumia Ventures, with Corewell Health Ventures, Y Combinator, Heuristic Capital and MassMutual Ventures, for sixteen million total. Kivo runs virtual home programs for chronic lung disease, starting with COPD. Patients get a kit with a tablet, a pulse oximeter and exercise bands, meet a respiratory therapist by video, and then join small group exercise and breathing classes twice a week for nine weeks. Kivo says it's covered by Medicare, Medicaid and major insurers, and claims it saves about eight thousand two hundred dollars per member per year by avoiding hospital stays and emergency visits. That savings figure is the company's own claim. Why this matters: notice the shape. The devices are cheap and simple, an oximeter and a tablet, and the value is in the human program built around them: a therapist, a group, a schedule. That's a pattern we'll come back to in the learning segment.

Third, PACE. We mentioned this bill on October nineteenth, and Home Health Care News now has the details. The PACE Access Improvement Act, from Michigan Representatives Debbie Dingell and John Moolenaar, would require every state's Medicaid program to offer PACE, which is currently available in thirty three states. It would let coverage start the day the paperwork is signed instead of the first of the next month, make it easier to open and expand PACE centers, and allow outreach before a center opens. A Senate companion led by Senator Mark Warner is expected in November. InnovAge, which runs PACE programs in Pennsylvania and five other states, says PACE reaches only a small fraction of people who could benefit. Why this matters: PACE is one of the few fully integrated, risk bearing models built around frail older adults, and if it grows, so does demand for the services around it, like enrollment, transportation, home care and in home safety.

On research and social, it was quiet. I couldn't reach Reddit or Hacker News directly, so nothing reliable to report.

The Resilient angle. Today's stories line up neatly. Medicare is about to make outside monitoring vendors much harder to pay for in traditional Medicare. The companies doing well, like Kivo, use simple devices inside a human program that a payer will cover for a clear clinical reason. And risk bearing models like PACE and Medicare Advantage are the buyers who care about outcomes. For Resilient, that suggests the device should be the quiet part of a program, not the product itself, and the first question for any new idea should be which program and which payer the device serves.

Today's learning segment is wearables for older adults: adoption, accuracy, and what actually sticks. Start with adoption. In a Pew Research survey this February, thirty seven percent of American adults said they own a smartwatch. By age, it was forty four percent of adults under fifty, thirty five percent of those fifty to sixty four, and twenty four percent of those sixty five and older. AARP's 2026 tech trends report found about thirty six percent of adults fifty and older own a wearable, versus forty four percent of younger adults, the biggest age gap of any device it tracked. So roughly a quarter of seniors already wear a smartwatch, and those owners skew younger, healthier and wealthier than the people care programs most want to reach. That's an inference, but it's a consistent finding in the literature.

Next, will older adults actually wear them? In research settings, yes, mostly. A 2025 study in JMIR Human Factors gave forty seven adults aged fifty five to eighty three, about one in five with mild cognitive impairment, a Garmin smartwatch to wear twenty three hours a day for four weeks. Average wear was about twenty one hours a day, with no drop over the month, and only one person quit, because the band was too tight. But the complaints are telling: battery life was the top problem, with thirty eight percent saying the watch died at least once; discomfort and technical issues came next; and people wanted bigger screens and different bands. Only fifty five percent said they'd keep wearing it without being asked. Black participants wore the watch slightly less, and the authors raised, without testing, the possibility of comfort or sensor accuracy on darker skin. Outside of studies, the general pattern from the literature is that many consumer wearables are abandoned within months, which I'm stating from general knowledge rather than a specific new study.

On accuracy: step counts and heart rate at rest are generally decent on modern devices, but accuracy drops with slow walking, walkers and canes, irregular rhythms, and movement. Optical sensors can be less accurate on darker skin and with poor circulation, an issue that also affects pulse oximeters and has drawn FDA attention. That's from memory. Fall detection is the feature families care most about, and the honest picture is that it misses some real falls, especially slow slides from a chair or bed, and produces false alarms that people learn to ignore. Atrial fibrillation detection is the best validated clinical feature on mainstream smartwatches.

So what sticks? Four things, in my reading of the evidence. First, a reason the wearer cares about, not just a family member: comfort, confidence, or a clear health goal. Second, low effort: long battery life, no daily charging if possible, nothing to sync or troubleshoot. Third, a human on the other end who responds, because data nobody acts on loses the wearer quickly. And fourth, a payer or program that funds the service, not just the device. Devices bolted onto nothing tend to end up in a drawer.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: a virtual group rehab program for older adults with heart and lung disease, starting in western Pennsylvania. The customer is a Medicare Advantage plan or risk bearing primary care group, and the patient is an older adult with COPD or heart failure who qualifies for pulmonary or cardiac rehab but never gets it. The problem: rehab works, but very few eligible patients attend, mostly because it means driving to a hospital gym two or three times a week for weeks, which is hard for frail older adults. Low participation rates are widely reported, but I'm citing that from memory. The service would deliver rehab at home: a therapist led video intake, small live group exercise and breathing classes, education for patient and family, and simple at home vital sign checks for safety during exercise. Who pays: Medicare covers pulmonary and cardiac rehab, and virtual delivery has been allowed under the telehealth flexibilities, which run through 2027, though rules on supervision need checking. Medicare Advantage plans and groups could also pay per completed program, tied to fewer hospital stays. Why now: Kivo's raise shows payers will cover it, the telehealth extension buys time, and readmission penalties and star ratings make the savings visible. Risks: Kivo and others are already in the market, safety supervision rules for exercise at home are strict, and older patients with limited technology skills will need hands on setup. On hardware, an honest note: a simple pulse oximeter or blood pressure cuff is genuinely useful for safe exercise at home, and a comfortable continuous monitor like Resilient's could add value during sessions, but the program, not the device, is the product. The first test is to call two western Pennsylvania pulmonary or cardiac rehab programs and ask what share of referred older patients actually complete rehab, and what stops the rest.

A question to think about today: of the older adults you know who own a smartwatch, how many would keep wearing it if nobody ever looked at the data? That's your briefing. Have a good Wednesday.
