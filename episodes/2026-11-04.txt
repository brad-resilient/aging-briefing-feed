And a look at home and community based services finds providers squeezed by Medicaid cuts, a federal policy reversal and the loss of immigrant workers. First, the election. As of early this morning, ABC News projects Democrats will win enough seats to control both the House and the Senate. Several Senate races were still being counted, with Democrats leading in Maine, Ohio and Texas, so treat the exact margins as preliminary. Here's what it likely means for aging and health care, and this part is my own analysis. Starting in January, Congress and the White House will be split. Democrats will probably push to delay or soften the Medicaid work requirements and the Medicaid cuts in last year's budget law, but any law still needs the president's signature, so big reversals are unlikely. What changes for sure is oversight. Expect hearings on Medicare Advantage billing, the star ratings fights, nursing home staffing and private equity in hospice and home care. Bipartisan bills, like the one to expand PACE programs, may actually have a better shot, because they're the kind of thing both sides can agree on. And the lame duck session between now and January could see a rush to pass health extenders before power shifts.

Second, Medicare Advantage and home health. Home Health Care News reports data presented at the home care industry's annual meeting from Homecare Homebase, a software company, and SimiTree, a consulting firm. Traditional Medicare pays home health agencies about two hundred sixty six dollars per visit on average. Medicare Advantage pays about two hundred six. Agencies spend about thirteen percent more office time per week on Medicare Advantage patients. Yet the patients get about the same number of visits and are rehospitalized at about the same rate. Medicare Advantage now makes up nearly half the patients in that dataset. Overall home health margins have fallen from about seven percent to about three percent, and one consultant said traditional Medicare is the only payer still producing a positive margin. Their advice to agencies: push Medicare Advantage plans toward per episode contracts like traditional Medicare, and bring outcome data to negotiations. Why this matters: as Medicare Advantage keeps growing, home health agencies are losing money on half their patients. Anything that helps an agency prove better outcomes, or cut its paperwork, has a buyer.

Third, home and community based services. Home Health Care News published a state of the industry piece. About eight point four million people get Medicaid home and community based services, and Medicaid paid more than half of the two hundred eighty six billion dollars spent on these services in 2024, according to Brandeis University. Providers face last year's budget law, which cuts about a trillion dollars from Medicaid over time; a 2024 federal rule requiring eighty percent of Medicaid home care payments to go to worker wages; a July Justice Department move that advocates call a reversal of federal policy favoring care at home over institutions for people with disabilities; and the end of Temporary Protected Status for Haitian immigrants, many of whom work as caregivers. Democrats introduced a bill in August to make these services mandatory under Medicaid. With Democrats taking Congress, that bill gets a hearing, but probably not a law. Why this matters: the home care workforce is shrinking at the same time demand rises, so anything that stretches a caregiver's time has real value.

Also: as of this morning, Medicare's final physician payment rule, with the remote patient monitoring changes, still hasn't published. The freeze on new home health agencies expires in nine days.

On the social side, I couldn't reach Reddit or Hacker News directly, so nothing reliable to report.

The Resilient angle. Two things. First, a Democratic Congress means more scrutiny of Medicare Advantage and of private equity in post acute care, which favors companies that can show clean data and real outcomes. Second, hospice. Hospices are under pressure to document that patients really are declining, and to avoid inappropriate live discharges. Objective trend data, like activity, sleep and vital signs over time, could help clinicians see decline earlier and plan the right care, including when someone is ready for hospice. But this is sensitive ground: the goal must be better care and timely conversations, never manufacturing eligibility. Separately, comfort care often involves opioids, and respiratory monitoring for people on opioids at home is an area close to Resilient's existing work.

Today's learning segment is end of life care and hospice. Hospice is a Medicare benefit created in 1983 for people with a life expectancy of six months or less who choose comfort care instead of curative treatment, which I'm citing from memory. Medicare pays the hospice a daily rate per patient, mostly for care at home, covering nursing visits, aides, social workers, chaplains, medications and equipment related to the terminal illness.

The numbers, from the Medicare Payment Advisory Commission's March report on 2024: about one point eight two million Medicare beneficiaries used hospice, and fifty three percent of Medicare decedents used it, a record. Medicare spent twenty eight point three billion dollars, up ten percent in a year. There were six thousand seven hundred hospices. About eighty two percent are for profit, and they serve sixty percent of patients. For profit hospices grew by about five percent while nonprofits shrank by seven percent. Private equity and publicly traded companies bought more than twelve hundred hospice agencies between 2010 and 2021.

Here's the economic engine. Medicare pays a flat daily rate, a little higher in the first sixty days, and the cost of care is highest at the start and in the last days of life. So long stays with stable patients are the most profitable. The median stay is only nineteen days, because many people enroll in their last week or two, but the average is almost one hundred days, pulled up by long stays, often for dementia. Hospice margins averaged eight percent in 2023: about fourteen percent for for profits and slightly negative for nonprofits. MedPAC recommended no payment increase for 2027, but Medicare finalized a two point three percent increase anyway. There's a cap on total payments per patient per year, about thirty six thousand dollars for fiscal 2027. In 2023, about twenty eight percent of hospices exceeded the cap, which is a red flag that they're enrolling people who aren't actually dying.

That leads to the controversies. Live discharge, when a patient leaves hospice alive, happened in about nineteen percent of cases. Some of that is normal, but high rates suggest enrolling ineligible patients. Los Angeles County became the epicenter: a California state audit found numerous signs of fraud, and a congressional investigation this spring cited estimates of three and a half billion dollars in fraudulent billing there. Medicare put new hospices in Arizona, California, Nevada and Texas under enhanced oversight and, by mid 2025, had revoked enrollment for one hundred twenty two of six hundred sixty eight hospices it reviewed. Medicare now gives every hospice a risk score based on spending and care patterns. There's also the opposite problem: many people who would benefit from hospice get it too late, or never, especially Black and rural patients, which I'm stating from general knowledge.

And there's a gap before hospice. People with serious illness who aren't ready to give up treatment often need palliative care, which is symptom management and planning while still being treated. Traditional Medicare has no dedicated benefit for home based palliative care. Medicare pays doctors for advance care planning conversations, from memory, but those conversations are still far too rare. Medicare Advantage plans and accountable care organizations, which save money when patients avoid unwanted hospital stays at the end of life, are the main buyers of palliative care programs today.

Finally, the opportunity of the day: a home based palliative care and advance care planning service for risk bearing organizations. The customer is a Medicare Advantage plan, an accountable care organization, including the new LEAD model starting in January, or a large home care company like BrightSpring taking on risk. The problem: in the last year of life, many seriously ill older people bounce between the emergency room, hospital and nursing home, often receiving care they wouldn't have chosen, and it's enormously expensive. The service identifies seriously ill members from claims data, sends trained nurses and social workers, with physician backup, to have advance care planning conversations, manage symptoms at home, run a phone line that's open around the clock so families call it instead of 911, and hand off smoothly to hospice when the time comes. Who pays: the plan or ACO, per member per month, with a share of savings. Why now: more people are in Medicare Advantage and ACOs that bear total cost risk, hospice is under fraud scrutiny so plans want trustworthy partners, and the new Congress will scrutinize end of life care quality. Risks: the incentive to save money near death must never override patient wishes, so outcomes should include patient and family satisfaction and goal concordant care; staffing nurses is hard; and competitors exist, such as Aspire Health and other palliative care groups, which I'm citing from memory. No hardware is needed, although symptom monitoring at home could help some patients. The first test: partner with one Pittsburgh area Medicare Advantage plan or ACO, enroll fifty seriously ill members, and measure emergency visits, hospital days in the last ninety days of life, and whether patients' care matched their stated wishes.

A question to think about: if hospices are paid the same daily rate whether a patient is stable or dying, what payment design would reward hospices for doing hard cases well, and who would push for it in a new Congress? That's your briefing. Have a good Wednesday.
